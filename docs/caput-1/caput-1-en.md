## On the Origin of Geometric Knowledge and Its Problematics

We begin with a very concise exposition of the historical development of the problems surrounding geometric knowledge, from Plato and Aristotle onward. In what follows, we will not presuppose the doctrine of Aristotle (or of the Scholastics); we shall only inquire whether within it are found principles and evidence that contain the solution to noetic problems—even modern problems. Nor do we wish to exclude interpretations of certain Aristotelian texts that differ from our own. But we are convinced that we have found in Aristotle illumination for the study of modern problems, and conversely, in the latter, the keys to interpreting Aristotle.

---

### § 1. On the Proper Place of this Investigation in Philosophy

Aristotle's general epistemology is set forth primarily in his *Analytics*, and for our specific subject, above all in the *Posterior Analytics*. These treat principally of "science" in the strict sense (*episteme*, ἐπιστήμη), which constructs apodictic demonstrations, and which is contradistinguished from "intellect" or intuitive understanding in the proper sense (*nous*, νοῦς), which apprehends the first principles from which demonstrations take their start. 

The *Prior Analytics* study the instruments of demonstration—that is, syllogisms according to their logical form alone; such a syllogism, even when formally "valid," can be constructed from premises that are neither certain nor true. The *Posterior Analytics*, by contrast, take as their proper object demonstration strictly so called, whose premises are true, certain, and necessary—that is, the syllogism that produces scientific knowledge in the strict sense. In this, not only the logical form, but also the matter and content must be examined. Here is a paraphrase of St. Thomas’s commentary (*Anal. Post.* I, lect. 6) expounding these distinctions.

Aristotle, in his investigations, demonstrates that in demonstration, the analysis (resolution) that examines the premises of a conclusion cannot be extended to infinity, and that a strictly circular demonstration is likewise invalid. Analysis must therefore discover primitive propositions: first principles, axioms, postulates, and hypotheses. This applies to propositions insofar as they are the constituent elements of syllogisms.

Something analogous must be said concerning the notions or concepts that are the elements of propositions: not all can be defined in terms of more familiar ones; here too one must arrive at primary, primitive notions.

In this manner, therefore, Aristotle examines the general architecture of the sciences, which consists in the connections established between primary notions and first principles, and the notions and propositions (truths) derived from them through syllogism and definition. He does not examine this structure in the same manner as a specialized science (such as geometry) does when constituting its own particular geometric system. The object of the *Analytics* is not the subject matter of the specialized science; its object is that science itself—its origin, its nature, and its structure. The *Analytics* therefore investigate the very operations of the human mind: not from a psychological standpoint, as mere mental acts, but from a noetic standpoint, examining how these acts attain their intentional objects. The *Analytics* are therefore a true theory of knowledge—even in this first part, which examines the general structure of scientific systems.

Yet they possess a second task, by which they constitute a theory of knowledge no less fundamentally. In this analysis of science, we arrive at first principles that, if the conclusions are to be apodictic, must themselves be certain and necessary.

Whence the process of "judicative logic" leads by its very nature to the fundamental problem: *whence arises in the human mind certain knowledge of principles, and whence is obtained certain knowledge of their necessity?* This constitutes the second great part of the inquiry of the *Posterior Analytics*.

Consequently, a twofold problem is treated in these books:
1. The problem of the **structure of science**, which arises from the nexus between principles and the conclusions derived from them;
2. The problem of **first principles** themselves.

It is clear that all these matters are not examined purely in the abstract—that is, detached from any concrete specialized science. This holds true especially for the second problem, which must primarily examine the matter and content of propositions, "because propositions that are per se and necessary are taken."

Hence Aristotle, in these investigations, almost always keeps before his eyes as the archetype of science: **geometry**. This is true both when he discusses demonstrative science in general, and—as is self-evident—in the very numerous cases where he draws his examples directly from geometry. St. Thomas notes this at the very beginning of his commentary (*Anal. Post.* I, lect. 1, n. 10):

> "[Aristotle] manifests the proposed premise through induction: first in demonstrative matters wherein science is acquired. Among these, however, the mathematical sciences are primary, on account of their most certain mode of demonstration."

The reason is manifest. In Aristotle's time, geometry was the sole demonstrative science that constituted a systematic body of doctrine. Barely a quarter of a century after the philosopher's death, Euclid composed his *Elements*, which for nearly twenty centuries were employed by humanity as an almost perfect textbook. But the doctrines set forth there had already been discovered and systematically organized during Aristotle's lifetime; their ultimate and greatest perfections had been achieved in the Academy of Plato, especially by Eudoxus and Theaetetus. It is no wonder, then, that Aristotle, in examining the nature of apodictic science, constantly looked to geometry.

The geometers themselves, in constructing their science, carried out a resolution back to first principles, which they did not demonstrate. These principles—the terminus of the geometers' resolution—were regarded in Plato's Academy as hypotheses (ὑποθέσεις) (*Rep.* VI, 510c), which were simply taken for granted by geometers; for "positing these, as manifest to all, they think they owe no further account of them." But philosophers demanded and sought the rational ground of these principles; and in Plato, it was precisely the office of the "dialectical method" to conduct this inquiry, and thus dialectic "destroys" or cancels (ἀναιρεῖ) (*Rep.* VII, 532c) these hypotheses by anchoring them in the unhypothetical first principle.

This doctrine reappears in a more developed form in Aristotle. In *Posterior Analytics* I, ch. 2, he distinguishes among the propositions that serve as principles of science: those that are axioms (ἀξιώματα, common notions, dignities) from those that are either hypotheses/suppositions (ὑποθέσεις) or postulates/petitions (αἰτήματα). The difference may be briefly stated thus: an axiom (ἀξίωμα) is immediately self-evident to anyone; the other propositions are not.

A principle of a specialized science, if it accords with the opinion of the learner, is a supposition (hypothesis); if not, it is a postulate [^1]. Plato had already spoken of the agreement (ὁμολογία) between teacher and student (*Rep.* VII, 533c). But all such principles must be received by the geometer from another, higher science—whether they are demonstrated in it by strict demonstration or established by the philosophical consideration of terms. This higher science, which replaces Platonic dialectic and from which the specialized science receives its principles, is according to St. Thomas either metaphysics (*Anal. Post.* I, lect. 5, n. 7; lect. 17, n. 4) or "natural philosophy" (*ibid.*, lect. 5, n. 7); but it is always philosophy.

It is clear that in Peripatetic philosophy, it is the proper office of the philosopher to pronounce upon the first principles of geometry—a fact that will become even more apparent in the following chapters—whether one attributes this task to metaphysics, to natural philosophy, or to a distinct branch of philosophy called noetics or epistemology.

The resolution of geometric propositions leads therefore to certain primary propositions. In carrying out this resolution itself, almost everything must be left to mathematicians; only general matters concerning the overarching structure of science fall to the philosopher, alongside the activity of the human mind in the individual operations that constitute the science, for these undoubtedly pertain to the epistemologist. But in examining the objective validity of principles, the primary responsibility falls upon philosophers.

We may pursue this inquiry toward a twofold end:
1. First, to demonstrate that all the foundations of a complete geometric science are justified, so that it becomes clear that an entire geometric science can be safely constructed upon them. We shall not strive for exhaustive completeness in these lectures, though we shall address the principal questions.
2. Second, to discover conclusions—and perhaps methods—of immense value for general epistemology.

One example we bring forward now, leaving the rest to be seen as the work unfolds. A general theory of knowledge cannot be constructed without examining the judgment of the human mind with respect to its meaning, its origin, and its validity. But to evaluate judgment itself, it is not sufficient to posit a general definition of judgment and analyze it abstractly; rather, certain determinate, concrete judgments must be actively elicited—they must arise in our living consciousness—and their origin and validity must be critically inspected [^2]. 

Just as we grasp universal and necessary judgments in concrete cases through mental intuition—examples will abound throughout our exposition—so too the universal theory of judgment itself shines forth from apt, determinate judgments. These are then considered not merely as judgments of a specialized science, but as specimens of judgment as such, revealing the nature of this mental operation to the mind itself. The examination of mathematical principles provides the very best specimens, if indeed it is true—as St. Thomas affirmed—that mathematics possesses the most certain mode of demonstration. For demonstration comprises not only formal validity, but also, and above all, the known necessity of the matter, and hence the necessity of principles. It is therefore intolerable for a philosopher working in epistemology to neglect these principles.

### § 2. On the Origin of Mathematical Notions

All these considerations must be urged in our time with greater urgency than ever before. To appreciate why, it will be helpful to trace briefly the history of the problem of mathematical knowledge.

Up to roughly the middle of the nineteenth century, the common conviction of philosophers, mathematicians, and indeed the entire human race was that mathematical knowledge is ultimately drawn from the data of sensibility—whether from the external senses or from the imagination. In further explaining this origin, opinions certainly diverged:
* For **Plato**, on account of the imperfection of sensibility, sensory data were merely the occasion or required preparation for the mind to recollect (*anamnesis*) what it had formerly seen by direct mental intuition (see *Meno* 82b–85b and elsewhere); and mathematical forms themselves could only be imperfectly participated in by sensible things.
* **Aristotle** maintained that "mathematicals" (τὰ ἐξ ἀφαιρέσεως, objects of abstraction) are acquired by abstraction from sensitive data, both with respect to concepts and with respect to first principles, which themselves are said to be known through a kind of induction (*epagoge*) [^3]. This does not mean that the mind's judgment merely mirrors what the senses report; rather, just as the agent intellect (*intellectus agens*) operates in abstracting ideas from sensory images, so too in the origin of those judgments that serve as first principles, there is an intellective operation that transcends sense and grasps essences. In the data of the imagination, the intellect directly inspects natures through mental intuition (*Anal. Post.* I, 12, 77b30): "these things [i.e., mathematicals] are, as it were, to see by intellection" (ταῦτα δ' ἐστὶν οἷον ὁρᾶν τῇ νοήσει). Cf. *De Anima* III, 8, 432a5–9 (St. Thomas, lect. 13; Pirotta n. 791); *ibid.* III, 7, 431a14–16; 431b2 (St. Thomas, lect. 12; Pirotta nn. 770–772, 777).
* By **Kant**, a subjective *a priori* form of outer sensibility is devised to explain the necessity of geometric judgments. We shall say more concerning this view later; for now, let it suffice to note that even Kant admits an intuition (though not an intellective one) through a phantasm, which is supposed to explain the origin of geometric knowledge.

These classical schools agreed on one essential point: **they affirmed both the necessity and the exactitude of mathematical knowledge**.

In the nineteenth century, however, **Empiricism**—especially as formulated by John Stuart Mill—fully admitted the origin of mathematical knowledge from sensory data, but on that very account equated mathematics entirely with empirical physical science. Consequently, Mill denied both the apodictic necessity and the absolute exactitude that humanity had always attributed to mathematical knowledge.

We shall easily dispose of this crude empiricist theory later as untenable; yet it effectively highlights two distinct problems that the epistemologist must resolve if mathematical knowledge is to be vindicated in its necessity and exactitude. This duty presses upon us today with urgent force—not because of Mill's empiricism, but because of modern mathematical theories that arose from these very difficulties. For these modern theories reject the classical doctrines in their entirety, and in doing so, radically subvert the centuries-old conviction of mankind concerning the objective value of geometry.

---

### § 3. The Twofold Fundamental Problem

Here, then, are the two fundamental problems:

#### 1. The Problem of Necessity
The data of sensibility—that which is known by the senses, insofar as it is known solely by them—are inherently **contingent**. The senses report only factual occurrences. Yet mathematical judgments present themselves as **absolutely necessary**, in arithmetic as well as in geometry. If these judgments originate from sensory data, *whence arises this absolute necessity?*

This first problem is common to both arithmetic and geometry.

#### 2. The Problem of Exactitude
Geometry carries with it a second problem that is uniquely its own: **sensory data are never exact**. Whether in the imaginative intuition of the spatial continuum or in external sensory perception—even when aided by instruments of the highest precision—exactitude is lacking:
* Senses never perceive an unextended point, but only tiny physical bodies that are not points.
* Senses cannot perceive a mathematical line, but only elongated bodies whose width is never zero.
* Senses cannot distinguish between a perfectly straight line and one that is imperceptibly curved.
* Senses cannot determine whether three lines intersect at one exact mathematical point or merely approximate concurrency.
* The imagination is similarly constrained.

For all sensory cognition, there exists a finite **threshold of exactitude** (*limen exactitudinis*) that the senses cannot transcend. And yet geometry claims to pronounce judgments that are **absolutely and infinitely exact**: it teaches, for instance, that the three medians of a triangle concur with infinite, mathematical precision at a single point. *Whence arises this exactitude?*

In arithmetic, this second problem does not arise: the senses can clearly perceive and verify whether three or five objects are present, and what results from adding these discrete collections; here exactitude is not lacking in the sensory data.

Not all philosophers have clearly distinguished this second problem. Kant seems to have overlooked it entirely. In modern mathematical epistemology, however, it plays the leading role, even if thinkers have not always formulated it with full clarity.

Modern theories have arisen largely from the difficulties surrounding this second problem. Regarding the problem of necessity—with one exception to be noted later—modern thinkers say little; they treat it as solved by describing "pure" mathematics as a pure creation of the human mind, in which case the problem of exactitude also appears to vanish. But then other urgent questions immediately arise:
* What is the real objective meaning of such a mind-constructed doctrine?
* How can it be applicable to the real physical world—to which scientists nevertheless apply it with astonishing precision?

It is no wonder that in grappling with this question, thinkers sometimes revert to a raw empiricism. Thus Albert Einstein famously observed:

> "How can it be that mathematics, being after all a product of human thought independent of experience, is so admirably appropriate to the objects of reality? Is human reason then, without experience, merely through pure thought, able to fathom the properties of real things?
>
> In my opinion, the brief answer to this question must be: *Insofar as the propositions of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain; and insofar as they are certain, they do not refer to reality.*" [^4]

Even setting aside any rhetorical exaggeration, such pronouncements clearly demand a fresh critique of the foundations of mathematics. They presuppose a lengthy historical development that has increasingly denied credibility to the imaginative intuition of the continuum—from which geometric knowledge historically sprang—and has even depicted that intuition as leading into positive error.

This modern evolution proceeded along two main pathways, both addressing the transition from sensory inexactitude to the exactitude of mathematical thought:
1. The **arithmetization of the continuum**;
2. The critical investigations arising from the critique of **Euclid's Fifth Postulate**.

Let us review this twofold evolution briefly [^5].

---

### § 4. On the Arithmetization of the Continuum

The ancient Greeks derived numbers from the contemplation of the continuous magnitude given in intuition. Aristotle, as is well known, distinguished between continuous quantity and discrete quantity, the latter arising from the division of the former. Multiplicity arises from division; multiplicity measured by unity is number: the integer or natural number.

How the notion of fractional number arose from this same division requires no lengthy exposition here. The Greeks, it seems, were unwilling to treat fractions as numbers in their own right; where the Egyptians (and moderns) treated a fraction as an autonomous number, the Greeks recognized ratios (*proportiones*) between continuous magnitudes—such as lines—that relate to one another as numbers. Thus, if lines of different lengths can be brought to equality by integral multiplication, their ratio is inversely proportional to those numbers.

Yet the Greeks soon discovered lines that admitted of no rational numerical ratio—magnitudes that were incommensurable and irrational, such as the side and diagonal of a square.

The profound crisis arising from these irrational (yet geometrically real) ratios was brilliantly resolved by Eudoxus through his theory of proportions, set forth in Book V of Euclid’s *Elements* [^6].

Modern mathematicians, certainly from Descartes onward, treated all these ratios as numbers. Just as the Greeks had a "continuum of ratios"—an *ensemble* of all continuous proportions—so the moderns developed the **continuum of real numbers**: an ensemble containing not only integers and fractions, but also irrational numbers (both algebraic and transcendental). Through these real numbers, modern mathematical analysis was constructed, bringing the continuum and continuous change under infinitesimal calculus. Yet these numbers and this analysis were originally abstracted from the intuitive continuum; they were simply another name for the Greek ratios.

In the course of the nineteenth century, serious objections were raised against this foundation. The intuitive continuum, plagued by the inexactitude of sensory perception, was judged unfit to serve as the rigorous ground for the system of real numbers. Consequently, mathematicians sought to deduce the continuum of real numbers purely from the arithmetic intuition of the discrete series of integers ($1, 2, 3, \dots$ *ad infinitum*), which suffers from no sensory threshold of inexactitude.

Few mathematicians described this dilemma with complete clarity. Two celebrated figures, however, spoke with unmistakable precision:

**Felix Klein**, in his work *Anwendung der Differential- und Integralrechnung auf Geometrie: Eine Revision der Prinzipien* (1907), addressed this problem *ex professo*. He clearly explained that in measurement through sensory perception (and in imagination), there is an insurmountable threshold of exactitude:

> "In all these practical realms there exists a threshold value of exactitude (*Schwellenwert der Genauigkeit*)." [^7]

In the arithmetic definition of a real number (as through an infinite decimal expansion), by contrast, no such threshold exists:

> "In the ideal realm of arithmetic there is no finite threshold value, as in the empirical realm; rather, the exactitude with which numbers are defined, or at least regarded as defined, is unlimited." [^8]

**Henri Poincaré** expressed the same shift with characteristic sharpness:

> "Intuition [of the continuum] can give us neither rigor nor even certainty; this has been recognized more and more." [^9]
>
> "We have therefore several kinds of intuition: first, the appeal to the senses and imagination; second, generalization by induction... and finally, we have the intuition of pure number... The first two cannot give us certainty; I have proved this above by examples; but who would seriously doubt the third—who would doubt Arithmetic?
>
> Now, in the Analysis of today, when one takes the trouble to be rigorous, there are only syllogisms and appeals to this intuition of pure number, the only one that cannot deceive us. It can be said that today absolute rigor has been attained." [^10]

In all this, Poincaré seems to overlook our **intellective intuition** of continuous extension—and he is far from alone among modern mathematicians in doing so. Yet it must be added that elsewhere Poincaré did not entirely reject geometric intuition in rigorous science, provided it is an intellective intuition. In *Dernières Pensées*, he sharply criticizes a certain purely topological definition:

> "This definition makes light of the intuitive origin of the notion of the continuum, and of all the riches that this notion conceals. It belongs to that class of definitions that have become so frequent in mathematics ever since the effort was made to 'arithmetize' the science. These definitions, mathematically irreproachable though they may be, cannot satisfy the philosopher." [^11]

Because these definitions disregard the intuitive origin of the continuum and its conceptual richness, they fail philosophically; and we would immediately add: **they render the application of such analysis to real extended things immensely difficult, if not impossible**. Note well what Poincaré adds:

> "I do not mean to say that this 'arithmetization' of mathematics is an evil; I say only that it is not the whole." [^12]

Arithmetization is indeed exceedingly useful; but precisely because it is not the whole, it is no surprise if it occasionally falls short—and if taken as the complete truth, it leads directly into error.

Modern analysis, constructed purely arithmetically from the series of integers, no longer depends upon the intuition of the continuum; on the contrary, it is used to construct an "abstract" geometry. And this was done, as we heard, because mathematicians lost faith in the continuum on account of sensory inexactitude. Indeed, they claimed to possess analytical examples (which we shall examine later) that positively contradict imaginative intuition.

If this is true, it presents the philosopher with a grave problem: *How was the human mind convinced for so many centuries of the absolute exactitude and supreme certainty of a geometry derived from such intuition?*

Some thinkers did not hesitate to declare that the classical method of constructing geometry was nothing but a dream, a "utopia." Thus the distinguished realist mathematician **Eduard Study**, in *Die realistische Weltansicht und die Lehre vom Raume* (1914, p. 131), wrote:

> "Decisive for judging the state of affairs seems to us to be the circumstance that a geometry truly independent of analysis, such as the ancient ideal properly demanded, has turned out to be a utopia." [^13]

Yet the difficulty becomes far more acute if further examination reveals that classical geometry cannot be constructed dependently upon analysis either! For in that case, the greatest mathematical geniuses up to the mid-nineteenth century were deceived not only in their method, but in the very truths they believed they had demonstrated.

And in fact, this appears to be precisely the case. If one speaks merely of approximate application (*Approximationsmathematik*, in Klein's phrase), no great difficulty arises, for this deals only with experimental physical bodies known through inexact sensory perception. But if one investigates the properties of **abstract extension**—the extended as extended (*extensum ut extensum*), of which classical geometry treated—these properties cannot be deduced from pure arithmetic analysis without all the same difficulties reappearing at the moment of application. And thus nothing has been gained.

If this is so, must we conclude that the human intellect cannot achieve perfect knowledge of extension—contrary to what mankind has always believed? What becomes of the Aristotelian epistemology, which held that this science originates from sensory data?

Moreover, shortly after the dawn of the twentieth century, internal difficulties arose within this proud arithmetic analysis itself, leading thinkers like Hermann Weyl and L. E. J. Brouwer to diagnose a veritable "foundational crisis" (*Grundlagenkrisis*) in mathematics. A few scholars, such as Otto Hölder, suggested that these foundational paradoxes can only be resolved by **returning to the consideration of the intuitive continuum**—a view that we believe to be entirely correct. 

This makes the task of investigating how the mind transcends the inexactitude of sensory data all the more urgent; and that is precisely what we shall attempt in the chapters that follow.

### § 5. On the Critique of Euclid's Fifth Postulate

The second pathway of modern mathematical philosophy was the critique of Euclid's Fifth Postulate—the Postulate of Parallels. 

The proposition formulated in this postulate is by no means immediately self-evident; it cannot be abstracted immediately from sensory data, precisely because of the threshold of inexactitude inherent in those data, as we shall see in due course.

(Remarkably, many mathematicians who label this postulate "less evident" than other axioms seem unable to identify the true root of this lack of self-evidence). 

The parallel postulate therefore belongs to those principles that, according to Aristotle, are not axioms (ἀξιώματα) but postulates (αἰτήματα). Consequently, they are admitted in geometry as working hypotheses (ὑποθέσεις), but must be explained and justified by another, prior science (*une science préalable*)—namely, philosophy, or what one may call "dialectics."

Already in antiquity, as Proclus testifies, doubts were raised concerning this postulate, and alternative formulations were proposed, all of which suffered from the same defect. The critique of the nineteenth century led to the conclusion—now universally accepted by mathematicians—that alongside classical Euclidean geometry, alternative geometries are logically possible that reject the Fifth Postulate and substitute an opposing premise (Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann).

Many—though not all—theorems deduced in these non-Euclidean systems contradict classical theorems; yet internal contradiction is entirely absent from each separate system, nor will any ever be found. Whence modern thinkers concluded: for centuries Euclidean geometry appeared to the human mind as uniquely possible and necessary; this is now shown to be false, for rival systems exist that are no less logically consistent. Here is another problem that general epistemology must resolve.

At first glance, it might seem that the necessity of geometry has been overthrown; and indeed some thinkers have classified geometry among the purely empirical physical sciences, which similarly lack internal necessity known *a priori*. This conclusion is demonstrably false: each geometric system is intrinsically necessary and is known by us as such. Nevertheless, the modern comparison between geometric and physical systems warrants our closest attention.

Yet a profound problem remains: the whole of humanity and all mathematicians—even those dissatisfied with the Fifth Postulate—regarded Euclidean geometry as uniquely necessary. If this was an error, how is it to be explained? If it was not an error, how are we to evaluate the rigorous modern critique? Here again is a momentous problem for the general theory of knowledge. When Aristotle and St. Thomas sought an example of an absolutely certain proposition, did they not unfailingly select theorems from Euclidean geometry?

### § 6. On So-Called Axiomatics

From this critique emerged an entire discipline within "judicative logic" known as **Axiomatics**. 

We have spoken of the intrinsic necessity and freedom from contradiction possessed by each geometric system, non-Euclidean as well as Euclidean. To establish these properties rigorously, the first principles of the science must be explicitly stated, each and every one. Once this is done, an entire system—such as Euclidean geometry—can be formulated as a single vast conditional proposition:
$$\text{If } A \text{ and } B \dots \text{ and } F, \text{ then } P \text{ and } Q \text{ and } R \dots$$
This proposition, as a genuine conditional, expresses intrinsic necessity. Such a science is **hypothetico-deductive**.

Many formalists maintain that pure geometry is nothing other than such a hypothetico-deductive system. The fundamental hypothesis—the set of axioms $A, B \dots F$—is no longer examined with regard to its objective, real truth; indeed, according to radical formalists, it is not even examined with regard to its meaning. Celebrated are the opening words with which David Hilbert begins Chapter 1 of *Die Grundlagen der Geometrie*:

> "We think of three different systems of things: the things of the *first* system we call *points* and designate them by letters $A, B, C, \dots$; the things of the *second* system we call *straight lines* and designate them by letters $a, b, c, \dots$; the things of the *third* system we call *planes* and designate them by letters $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \dots$" [^14]

Neither the objective reality nor the intuitive meaning of the terms matters; formal axiomatics requires only that from the posited premises, deduction proceed according to pure formal logic through valid syllogisms.

This position may at first glance seem quite Aristotelian, for Aristotle also held that science in the strict sense—as opposed to the intuitive understanding of principles—concerns conclusions logically deduced from premises. Yet a momentous difference exists, as we shall see in due course.

If the intrinsic meaning of axioms is ignored, it is obvious that one cannot speak of their self-evidence. Consequently, we cannot know *a priori* whether a given set of axioms will lead to a contradiction. Formal axiomatics must therefore devote itself to devising methods for proving that no contradiction will ever arise.

This formalist approach has generated both serious problems and valuable results:

#### The Problem
The primary problem is that which always recurs when we abandon the natural path of human thought (*le cheminement de la pensée*). The human mind is convinced that it knows objective extension, and that a science of supreme certainty can be constructed concerning this real object. A purely formal, non-contradictory axiomatic system does not attain this object of itself. To apply it to reality, the entire classical problem returns: *how does the mind pass from inexact (and contingent) sensory data to exact, necessary knowledge?* Classical geometry was not discovered as a mere hypothetico-deductive game.

#### The Precious Achievements
Yet axiomatics has also achieved results of immense value:
1. **Proofs of Consistency**: Starting from axioms whose self-evidence is bracketed, arbitrary systems might easily harbor hidden contradictions. Axiomaticians devised methods—often of astonishing ingenuity—to demonstrate that a specified system is completely free from internal contradiction, even in its most distant deductions. This represents an authentic advance in logic.
2. **Structural Analysis of Science**: These methods permit rigorous investigation into the particular architecture of scientific systems, carrying forward what Aristotle initiated in the *Posterior Analytics*. Not all geometric conclusions require the entire set of axioms for their proof; modern axiomatics determined precisely which axioms are required for which branches of the science, revealing its internal anatomy with unprecedented clarity.
3. **Axiomatic Independence**: Closely linked to consistency is the determination of the mutual independence of axioms, bringing further enrichment to judicative logic.
4. **Ontological Implications**: The method of proving non-contradiction leads directly to an ontological question concerning the theory of *possibilia*: possible beings are defined as objects whose notes involve no mutual contradiction. Can this theory be applied to systems proved consistent by axiomatics, such as non-Euclidean geometry? And more broadly, what does this imply for the problem of **mathematical existence**, concerning which Aristotle and St. Thomas formulated a doctrine that still awaits full modern development?

---

### Conclusion and Call to Action

From this brief survey, one conclusion stands out: **in this vital branch of philosophy, we note with profound sorrow the near-total absence of Scholastic investigations**. 

Yet this department of "judicative logic," so brilliantly inaugurated by Aristotle, urgently demands renewal, especially in the wake of the modern mathematical critique. It touches the gravest problems of epistemology—both special and general—the core problems of logic, and fundamental questions regarding the structure and cognitive activity of science.

Our goal is not to construct an exhaustive, technical axiomatization of geometry; that task belongs to mathematicians. Our attention must focus on the primary epistemological problems, for these concern first principles—a matter purely philosophical. We will not require advanced higher mathematics; elementary geometric concepts will suffice to reveal the deepest truths concerning human cognition.
