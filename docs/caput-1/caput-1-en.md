# CHAPTER I

## ON THE ORIGIN OF GEOMETRIC KNOWLEDGE
## AND ITS PROBLEMATICS

We begin with a very concise exposition of the historical development of the problems surrounding geometric knowledge, from Plato and Aristotle onward. In what follows, we will not presuppose the doctrine of Aristotle (or of the Scholastics); we shall only inquire whether within it are found principles and evidence that contain the solution to noetic problems, even modern problems. Nor do we wish to exclude interpretations of certain Aristotelian texts that differ from our own. But we are convinced that we have found in Aristotle illumination for the study of modern problems, and conversely, in the latter, the keys to interpreting Aristotle.

## § 1. ON THE PROPER PLACE OF THIS INVESTIGATION IN PHILOSOPHY.

Aristotle's general epistemology is set forth primarily in his *Analytics*, and for our specific subject, above all in the *Posterior Analytics*. These treat principally of "science" in the strict sense (*episteme*, ἐπιστήμη), which constructs apodictic demonstrations, and which is contradistinguished from "intellect" or intuitive understanding in the proper sense (*nous*, νοῦς), which apprehends the first principles from which demonstrations take their start. 

The *Prior Analytics* study the instruments of demonstration, that is, syllogisms according to their logical form alone; such a syllogism, even when formally "valid," can be constructed from premises that are neither certain nor true. The *Posterior Analytics*, by contrast, take as their proper object demonstration strictly so called, whose premises are true, certain, and necessary, that is, the syllogism that produces scientific knowledge in the strict sense. In this, not only the logical form, but also the matter and content must be examined. Here is a paraphrase of St. Thomas's commentary (*Anal. Post.* I, lect. 6) expounding these distinctions.

Aristotle, in his investigations, demonstrates that in demonstration, the analysis (resolution) that examines the premises of a conclusion cannot be extended to infinity, and that a strictly circular demonstration is likewise invalid. Analysis must therefore discover primitive propositions: first principles, axioms, postulates, and hypotheses. This applies to propositions insofar as they are the constituent elements of syllogisms.

Something analogous must be said concerning the notions or concepts that are the elements of propositions: not all can be defined in terms of more familiar ones; here too one must arrive at primary, primitive notions.

In this manner, therefore, Aristotle examines the general architecture of the sciences, which consists in the connections established between primary notions and first principles, and the notions and propositions (truths) derived from them through syllogism and definition. He does not examine this structure in the same manner as a specialized science (such as geometry) does when constituting its own particular geometric system. The object of the *Analytics* is not the subject matter of the specialized science; its object is that science itself, its origin, and its structure. The *Analytics* therefore investigate the very operations of the human mind: not from a psychological standpoint, as mere mental acts, but from a noetic standpoint, examining the ways by which these acts attain their intentional objects. The *Analytics* are therefore a theory of knowledge, even in this first part, which examines the general structure of scientific systems.

Yet they possess a second task, by which they constitute a theory of knowledge no less fundamentally. In this analysis of science, we arrive at first principles that, if the conclusions are to be apodictic, must themselves be certain and necessary.

Whence the process of "judicative logic" leads by its very nature to the fundamental problem: whence arises in the human mind certain knowledge of principles, and whence is obtained certain knowledge of their necessity? This constitutes the second part of the inquiry of the *Posterior Analytics*.

Consequently, a twofold problem is considered in these books: first, the problem of the structure of science, which arises from the connections between principles and the truths derived from them; second, the problem of first principles themselves.

It is clear that all these matters are not examined purely in the abstract, that is, in such a way that no specialized science is examined. This holds true especially for the second problem, which must primarily examine the matter of propositions, "because propositions that are per se and necessary are taken."

Hence Aristotle, in these investigations, almost always keeps before his eyes as the archetype of science: geometry. This is true both when he discusses demonstrative science in general, and, as is self-evident, in the very numerous cases where he draws his examples directly from geometry. St. Thomas notes this at the very beginning of his commentary (*Anal. Post.* I, lect. 1, n. 10):

> "[Aristotle] manifests the proposed premise through induction: first in demonstrative matters wherein science is acquired. Among these, however, the mathematical sciences are primary, on account of their most certain mode of demonstration."

The reason is manifest. In Aristotle's time, geometry was the sole demonstrative science that constituted a systematic body of doctrine. Barely a quarter of a century after the philosopher's death, Euclid composed his *Elements*, which for nearly twenty centuries were employed by humanity as an almost perfect textbook. But the doctrines set forth there had already been discovered and systematically organized during Aristotle's lifetime; their ultimate and greatest perfections had been achieved in the Academy of Plato, especially by Eudoxus and Theaetetus. It is no wonder, then, that Aristotle, in examining the nature of apodictic science, constantly had geometry before his eyes.

The geometers themselves, in constructing their science, carried out a resolution back to first principles, which they no longer demonstrated. These principles, the terminus of the geometers' resolution, were regarded in Plato's Academy as hypotheses (ὑποθέσεις) (*Rep.* VI, 510c), which were simply admitted by geometers; for "positing these, as manifest to all, they think they owe no further account of them." But philosophers demanded and sought the rational ground of these principles; and in Plato, it was precisely the office of the "dialectical method" to conduct this inquiry, and thus dialectic cancels (ἀναιρεῖ) (*Rep.* VII, 532c) these hypotheses.

This doctrine reappears in a more developed form in Aristotle. In *Posterior Analytics* I, ch. 2, he distinguishes among the propositions that serve as principles of science: those that are axioms (ἀξιώματα, common notions, dignities) from those that are either hypotheses or suppositions (ὑποθέσεις) or postulates or petitions (αἰτήματα). The difference may be briefly stated thus: an axiom (ἀξίωμα) is immediately evident to anyone; the other propositions are not.

A principle of a specialized science, if it accords with the opinion of the learner, is a supposition; if not, it is a postulate [^1]. Plato had already spoken of the agreement (ὁμολογία) between teacher and student (*Rep.* VII, 533c). But all such principles must be received by the geometer from another, higher science, whether they are demonstrated in it by strict demonstration or established by the consideration of terms. This higher science, which replaces Platonic dialectic and from which the specialized science receives its principles, is according to St. Thomas either metaphysics (*Anal. Post.* I, lect. 5, n. 7; lect. 17, n. 4) or "natural philosophy" (*ibid.*, lect. 5, n. 7); but it is always philosophy.

It is clear that in Peripatetic philosophy, it is the proper office of the philosopher to pronounce upon the first principles of geometry (a fact that will become even more apparent in the following chapters), whether one attributes this task to metaphysics, to natural philosophy, or to a distinct branch of philosophy called the theory of knowledge or noetics.

The resolution of geometric propositions leads therefore to certain primary propositions; and in carrying out this resolution itself, almost everything must be left to the mathematicians. Only general matters concerning the overarching structure of science fall to the philosopher, alongside the activity of the human mind in the individual operations that constitute the science, for these undoubtedly pertain to the epistemologist. But in examining the validity of principles, the primary office falls upon philosophers. This we can pursue for a twofold end. The first end is to demonstrate that all the foundations of a complete geometric science are genuinely justified, so that it becomes clear that an entire geometric science can be safely constructed upon them; we shall not strive for such complete exhaustiveness in our lectures, though we shall treat the principal questions.

The second end of this philosophical inquiry is that we shall discover conclusions (and perhaps methods) of great value for general epistemology. One example we bring forward now, leaving the rest to be seen as the work unfolds. A theory of knowledge cannot be constructed without examining the judgment of the human mind in relation to its meaning, its origin, and its validity. But to evaluate judgment itself, it is not sufficient to posit a general definition of judgment and resolve it abstractly; rather, certain determinate judgments must be actively elicited (they must therefore arise in our mind), and these judgments must be considered as to their origin and validity [^2]. Just as we inspect these very necessary and universal judgments in concrete cases through mental intuition (examples will abound throughout our exposition), so too the universal theory of judgment itself shines forth from apt, determinate judgments. These are then considered not only as judgments of a specialized science, but as specimens of judgment itself as such, revealing the nature of this operation of the human mind to the mind itself. The examination of mathematical principles will provide the very best specimens, if indeed it is true, as we heard St. Thomas saying, that this science possesses the most certain mode of demonstration. For demonstration comprises not only formal rectitude, but also and especially the known necessity of the matter, and hence the necessity of principles. Whence it is wrong for a philosopher who applies himself to the theory of knowledge to neglect these principles.

## § 2. ON THE ORIGIN OF MATHEMATICAL NOTIONS.

All these considerations must be urged in our time with greater urgency than ever before. To appreciate why, it will be helpful to recount very briefly the history of the problem of mathematical knowledge.

Up to roughly the middle of the nineteenth century, the common conviction of philosophers, mathematicians, and indeed the entire human race was that mathematical knowledge is ultimately drawn from the data of sensibility, whether from the external senses or from the imagination. In further explanation, to be sure, there was a diversity of opinions. For Plato, on account of the imperfection of sensibility, sensory data were merely the occasion or required preparation for the mind to recollect what it had formerly seen by mental intuition (see *Meno* 82b-85b and elsewhere); and mathematical forms themselves could only be imperfectly participated in by sensible things. Aristotle maintained that "mathematicals" (τὰ ἐξ ἀφαιρέσεως, objects of abstraction) are acquired by abstraction from sensitive data, both with respect to notions and with respect to first principles, which themselves are said to be known through a kind of induction (*epagoge*) [^3]. This does not mean that the mind's judgment merely mirrors what the senses report; rather, just as in the abstraction of ideas from sensitive data there is an operation of the agent intellect (*intellectus agens*), so too in the origin of those judgments that serve as first principles, there is a similar intellective operation that transcends sense and grasps nature. In the data of the imagination, the mind directly inspects natures through mental intuition (*Anal. Post.* I, 12, 77b30): "these things [i.e., mathematicals] are, as it were, to see by intellection" (ταῦτα δ' ἐστὶν οἷον ὁρᾶν τῇ νοήσει). Cf. *De Anima* III, 8, 432a5-9 (St. Thomas, lect. 13; Pirotta n. 791); *ibid.* III, 7, 431a14-16; 431b2 (St. Thomas, lect. 12; Pirotta nn. 770-772, 777).

By Kant, a subjective *a priori* form of outer sensibility is devised to explain the necessity of geometric knowledge.

Concerning this view we shall say more later; for now, let it suffice to note that even in this view an intuition (though not an intellective one) through a phantasm is admitted, which is supposed to explain the origin of geometric knowledge.

These schools therefore agree in admitting the necessity and exactitude of mathematical knowledge. In the preceding century, empiricism, especially as set forth by John Stuart Mill, fully admitted the origin of mathematical knowledge from the data of the senses, but on that account equated it entirely with physical knowledge, so that it denied both apodictic necessity and all-around exactitude, which mankind had always attributed to mathematical knowledge.

This empiricist theory we shall easily dispatch later as untenable; yet it effectively indicates two problems whose solution the epistemologist must find if he wishes to vindicate necessity and exactitude for mathematical knowledge. This is especially urgent today, not on account of that empiricist theory, but on account of modern theories that take their origin from these difficulties. For these theories reject the classical doctrines in their entirety, and by that very fact thoroughly overthrow the centuries-old conviction that mankind maintained concerning the value of geometric science.

The duty of examining these problems is therefore indisputably pressing.

## § 3. THE TWOFOLD FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEM.

Here are the two problems. The first is this: The data of sensibility, that is, those things known by the senses and insofar as they are known solely in that way, are of themselves contingent; the senses report only facts. Yet the judgments of mathematics present themselves as absolutely necessary, in arithmetic as well as in geometry. If those judgments must arise from sensitive data, whence arises that necessity?

This problem is found, as has been said, both in geometry and in arithmetic. But geometry alone carries with it another problem: sensory data regarding the continuum of imaginative intuition, and regarding the perceptions of the external senses (even with perfect instruments employed), are not exact. The senses do not perceive points without extension, but only small corpuscles that are not points; similarly they cannot perceive lines, but only elongated bodies whose width is not zero. The senses cannot distinguish between a straight line and another that is only slightly curved; they cannot decide whether three lines are almost or entirely concurrent; nor can the imagination distinguish all these things. For all sensitive knowledge there is a "threshold of exactitude" (*limen exactitudinis*) that the senses cannot cross. And yet geometry claims to proffer absolutely exact judgments concerning such things. It teaches that three median lines in a triangle concur entirely ("infinitely") exactly. Whence this exactitude? In arithmetic this problem is not present: the senses can perfectly perceive and decide whether three or five objects are present, and what results from the addition of these multitudes; there exactitude in sensitive data is not lacking.

Not everyone describes this second problem well; Kant seems not to have attended to it at all. In modern problematics it occupies the chief place, although, as we shall see, not everyone has been able to express it clearly in every problem.

Modern theories therefore arise especially from the difficulties of the second problem. Of the problem of necessity (except in one single case of which later) they speak little; it seems to be considered as solved from the fact that they describe "pure" mathematics as a pure "creation" of the human mind, in which opinion the problem of exactitude also seems to disappear. But then other questions naturally arise: the question of the real meaning of such a doctrine created by the human mind, and the question of its applicability to the real world, to which scientists nonetheless apply this doctrine.

Nor is it surprising that in this question the crude opinion of empiricism occasionally returns. Thus Einstein wrote:

> "How can it be that mathematics, being after all a product of human thought independent of experience, is so admirably appropriate to the objects of reality? Is human reason then, without experience, merely through pure thought, able to fathom the properties of real things?
>
> To this question, in my opinion, the brief answer must be: *insofar as the propositions of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain; and insofar as they are certain, they do not refer to reality.*" [^4]

Such pronouncements, even setting aside purely rhetorical exaggeration, clearly demand a fresh examination of the foundations of mathematics. They presuppose a lengthy evolution of doctrines that more or less deny credibility to the imaginative intuition of the continuum, from which geometric knowledge nonetheless arises, and indeed depict it as leading into error.

This modern evolution has progressed especially along a twofold path, and each path addresses the problem of the transition from the inexactitude of sensation to the exactitude of mathematical intellect. The first path leads to what is called the arithmetization of the continuum; the second leads to the doctrines that took their origin from the critique of Euclid's Fifth Postulate. Let us review this evolution briefly [^5].

## § 4. ON THE ARITHMETIZATION OF THE CONTINUUM.

The Greeks deduced numbers from the consideration of the continuous given in intuition. Aristotle, as is well known, distinguished between continuous quantity and discrete quantity, which takes its origin from the division of the former. Multiplicity arises from division; multiplicity measured by unity is number: the integer or natural number.

How the notion of fractional number arose from this same division requires no explanation here. The Greeks, as it seems, did not wish to consider fractions as numbers; where the Egyptians (and moderns) consider a fraction and indeed as a number, the Greeks admitted proportions (ratios) between continuous magnitudes, lines, which relate to one another as numbers. Thus, if lines of different lengths can be brought to equality by multiplication through different numbers, their ratio is inversely proportional to these numbers.

Yet they also discovered that there are lines (such as the side and the diagonal of a square) that admitted of no numerical ratio, which were incommensurable and irrational.

The difficulties arising from these irrational (yet real) proportions were brilliantly solved by Eudoxus through his theory of proportions, which is described in Book V of Euclid's *Elements* [^6].

More modern thinkers, certainly from Descartes onward, considered all these proportions as numbers. Just as the Greeks had a "continuum of proportions," which is the ensemble (*insieme*) of all proportions in the continuum, so the moderns have a continuum of numbers, the ensemble of "real numbers," which contains not only integers, but also fractional numbers, and irrational numbers as well (both algebraic and transcendental). By means of these numbers, modern "analysis" could be constructed, which, through the infinitesimal calculus, can subject the continuum, and even continuous changes, to calculation. But these numbers and this analysis were deduced from the intuitive continuum; these numbers are nothing but another name for the proportions of the Greeks.

At this point, in the course of the preceding century, difficulties were raised. The intuitive continuum, on account of the lack of exactitude in sensitive perception, did not seem to be a fit medium from which the body of real numbers could be deduced; whence mathematicians attempted to deduce this in another way, from the pure arithmetic intuition of the series of integers ($1, 2, 3, \dots$ to infinity), which does not labor under the defect of inexactitude.

Few describe this difficulty with precision; here are two celebrated names of learned men who speak with complete clarity.

Felix Klein, in his book *Anwendung der Differential- und Integralrechnung auf Geometrie: Eine Revision der Prinzipien* (which, as the second part of the title indicates, is entirely about this problem), treats this difficulty *ex professo*. He explains well that in measurement through the perception of the senses (and also in the imagination) there is a threshold of exactitude beyond which we cannot perceive:

> "In all these practical realms there exists a threshold value of exactitude (*Schwellenwert der Genauigkeit*)." [^7]

In the arithmetic definition of a real number (for example, by means of a decimal fraction), by contrast, no such threshold exists:

> "In the ideal realm of Arithmetic there is no finite threshold value, as in the empirical realm; rather, the exactitude with which numbers are defined, or at least regarded as defined, is unlimited." [^8]

We omit a certain difficulty that we have, as well as the manner in which Klein then proceeds in the construction of analysis (and of abstract, precise geometry, *Präzisionsmathematik*).

Let us hear also the words of Henri Poincaré:

> "Intuition [of the continuum] cannot give us exactitude, nor even certainty; this has been noticed more and more." [^9]
>
> "We have therefore diverse kinds of intuition: first, an appeal to the senses and to the imagination; next, generalization by means of induction... finally, we have the intuition of pure number... The first two cannot give us certainty; I have shown this above by examples; but who would seriously doubt the third, who would doubt Arithmetic?
>
> Yet in the analysis of today, if one wishes to bind oneself to rigor, there is found nothing but the syllogism and an appeal to this intuition of pure number, which alone cannot deceive us. It can be said that absolute exactitude is attained today." [^10]

In all this, Poincaré seems to neglect our intellective intuition of continuous extension; nor is he alone among mathematicians in doing so. It must be added, however, that elsewhere he does not entirely reject our geometric intuition in rigorous science, where there will be an intellective intuition. In *Dernières Pensées* he criticizes a certain topological definition, saying that:

> "this definition makes light of the intuitive origin of the notion of the continuum, and of all the riches that are contained in this notion. It belongs to the type of those definitions that occur so frequently in mathematics, after mathematicians attempted to 'arithmetize' this science. Those definitions, irreproachable from a mathematical point of view, as we were saying, cannot satisfy the philosopher." [^11]

Because they neglect the intuitive origin of the notion of the continuum, and consequently its "riches," they are deficient from a philosophical point of view; we would immediately add that they also render the application of analysis thus constituted to real extended things difficult, not to say impossible. But let us also attend to what Poincaré adds immediately: "I do not wish to say that this 'arithmetization' is an evil; I say that it is not the whole." [^12] It will be extremely useful, but because it is not the whole, it will not be surprising if it occasionally falls short, and then, if it is considered as the "whole," it will be able to lead into errors.

Analysis therefore, because it is constructed purely arithmetically starting from the series of integers, no longer depends, as before, upon the intuition of the continuum, but is conversely employed to construct a certain "abstract" geometry. And this was done, as we heard, because thinkers denied credibility to the intuition of the continuum on account of the inexactitude of sensitive data. Indeed, they think they have certain examples (we shall examine them later) that are deduced from analysis and are said to contradict imaginative intuition positively.

If these things are true, they constitute for the philosopher a most grave problem: whence could it happen that the human mind was for so many centuries so firmly persuaded of the exact value and supreme certainty of geometry deduced from such intuition?

There are not lacking those who therefore affirm that this classical method of constructing geometry, notwithstanding the greatest genius of many who used it, was nothing but a dream, "a utopia." Thus the celebrated "realist" mathematician Eduard Study, in his book entitled *Die realistische Weltansicht und die Lehre vom Raume* (Braunschweig 1914, p. 131), says:

> "Decisive for judging the state of affairs seems to us to be the circumstance that a geometry truly independent of analysis, such as the ancient ideal properly demanded, has turned out to be a utopia." [^13]

Yet the difficulty becomes still graver if further examination shows that this classical geometry cannot be constructed from analysis and dependently upon it either. For in such a case, mathematicians of the greatest genius up to the middle of the preceding century would have erred not only in method, but also in the very truths they thought they had discovered.

And indeed, in the case we suppose, the matter seems to stand precisely thus. Certainly, if it is a question of approximate application (*Approximationsmathematik* according to Felix Klein), there is no great difficulty; for this deals only with experimental extended things as they are known by the senses through inexact sensitive perception. But if it is a question of the properties of abstract extension, the extended as extended, about which classical geometry argued, this cannot, as it seems to us, be deduced from analysis without all the same difficulties appearing once again in this very application. And thus we have made no progress.

If that is true, must we not affirm that the human intellect does not even know this extension perfectly, which mankind hitherto has always believed? What in particular must be said of the Aristotelian theory of knowledge, which firmly maintained that this science arises from sensitive data?

There is something further. Shortly after the beginning of this century, difficulties arose within this very proud arithmetic analysis itself, which according to some (such as Weyl, Brouwer, and others) lead to a veritable "crisis" in mathematics. And there are some (for example, the distinguished Otto Hölder) who think the difficulties cannot be solved except by a return to the consideration of the intuitive continuum; which seems to us to be true. All the more urgent is the duty of inquiring into the way of overcoming the inexactitude of sensitive data; which we shall attempt to do in what follows.

## § 5. ON THE CRITIQUE OF EUCLID'S FIFTH POSTULATE.

The second path along which the modern evolution of the philosophy of mathematics has progressed was the critique of Euclid's Fifth Postulate, or the postulate of a unique parallel. The principle expressed by this postulate is certainly not immediately evident, and cannot be immediately abstracted from sensitive data, once again on account of the defect of exactitude in these data, as we shall see later in its proper place.

(It is strange: very many mathematicians, who call this principle "less evident" than other axioms, seem unable to indicate this root of the lack of evidence). It belongs therefore to those principles that according to Aristotle are not axioms (ἀξιώματα) but postulates (αἰτήματα); which accordingly are admitted in geometry as hypotheses (ὑποθέσεις), but must be explained and justified from another science, "a prior science," by philosophy; which, if you wish, you can call "dialectics."

Already in antiquity, as Proclus testifies, difficulties were moved regarding this postulate, and in place of it others were proposed, which nevertheless labored under the same defect. The critique of the preceding century led to this conclusion, at least now generally admitted by mathematicians: besides classical, Euclidean geometry, another is possible, which rejects the Fifth Postulate and introduces another in its place, opposite to it. Many theorems (not of course all) deduced from it contradict classical theses; but internal contradiction in the individual geometric systems is absent, nor will it be able to be found in the future. Thence they conclude: for many centuries Euclidean geometry seemed to the human mind to be the uniquely possible, necessary one; this now appears to be false; next to it other systems are present, which are no less possible. We fall into a problem that must be solved by the theory of knowledge.

At first glance, necessity seems to be denied to geometry; and indeed there are some who in this sense already number geometry among the purely physical sciences, which in a similar manner lack necessity perceived by us. This is without any doubt false, as can easily be shown: the individual systems are intrinsically necessary, and are known by us as such. But this comparison between geometric and physical sciences will be worthy of our attention.

Yet another most grave problem remains: the entire human race and all mathematicians, even those who were not content with the Fifth Postulate, acknowledged Euclidean geometry as uniquely necessary. If this is an error, how is it to be explained? If it is not an error, what is to be said of the severe modern critique? It is clear: once again it is a matter of a grave problem that has great importance in the general theory of knowledge. When Aristotle and St. Thomas wish to propose an absolutely certain thesis as an example, do they not choose theorems from Euclidean geometry?

## § 6. ON SO-CALLED AXIOMATICS.

From this critique an entire science has developed, a branch of "judicative logic," which they call Axiomatics. Above we spoke of the intrinsic necessity and freedom from internal contradiction that belongs to the individual geometric systems, even non-Euclidean ones. In order that these properties may be established, the first principles of such a science must genuinely be enunciated, each and every one. When this is done, one system, such as Euclidean geometry, can be enunciated as a single vast conditional proposition. Let us indicate the principles by the letters $A, B \dots$ and $F$, and then the conclusions by the letters $P, Q, R \dots$ The whole of geometry can now be enunciated thus: If $A$ and $B \dots$ and $F$, then $P$ and $Q$ and $R$ and $\dots$ This proposition, as a true conditional, enunciates intrinsic necessity. And this science will be hypothetico-deductive. Many axiomaticians maintain that pure geometry is nothing but such a hypothetico-deductive system. The fundamental hypothesis, that is, the series of axioms $A, B \dots F$, is no longer examined with respect to the real value of the propositions; indeed, according to extreme axiomaticians, not even with respect to their meaning. Well known are the words with which David Hilbert begins his exposition in the first chapter of the book *Die Grundlagen der Geometrie*:

> "We think of three different systems of things: the things of the *first* system we call *points* and designate them by letters $A, B, C, \dots$; the things of the *second* system we call *straight lines* and designate them by letters $a, b, c, \dots$; the things of the *third* system we call *planes* and designate them by letters $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \dots$" [^14]

Therefore neither the objective value nor the meaning matters; they require only that from the premised hypothesis, deduction take place according to pure formal logic through correct syllogisms.

This position at first glance can seem sufficiently Aristotelian; for also according to the philosopher, science strictly so called, insofar as it is opposed to the intellect of principles, purely regards logically deduced conclusions. Yet we shall see in its proper place that a massive distinction is present.

Now, if one does not care about the meaning of the axioms, it is clear that there can be no question of the evidence of the axioms.

Whence we cannot be certain *a priori* that no contradiction will follow from such a system of axioms. Whence axiomatics applies itself to finding methods to prove that no contradiction will follow.

This position leads both to problems and to valuable results. The first problem is the same one that always returns if we abandon the natural path along which the human mind proceeds (*le cheminement de la pensée*). The human mind thinks that it knows objective extension very well, and that concerning such an object a most certain science can be constructed. Such an axiomatic system, non-contradictory though it be, does not attain this object without more; for application to be made, the entire classical problem seems to return concerning the transition from inexact (and perhaps contingent) sensitive data to exact and necessary science. For classical geometry was not discovered solely through a hypothetico-deductive system.

Yet axiomatics has also discovered valuable results. As has already been said, it begins from a system of "axioms" concerning whose evidence it presupposes nothing, and therefore a system could without any doubt be established from which, quickly or after a longer deduction, a contradiction would follow. That deduction may be safe, it is therefore required that this be guarded against. And they investigated and discovered methods of demonstrating that a determinate system is free from contradiction even in future conclusions. Such methods, sometimes most ingenious, are without doubt a valuable development of logic.

Moreover, these methods permit investigations into the particularities of the structure of science, just as Aristotle did in the *Posterior Analytics* with respect to general structure. Not all conclusions require for their deduction the entire series of axioms; and it could be determined in various cases how the system of axioms can be distinguished into various parts that influence various parts of the science. Thus the structure of science can be known more distinctly. We have a further development of the doctrine of Aristotle. Connected with this question and with the problem of non-contradiction are methods of investigating the true mutual independence of the axioms that are proposed; and these also bring gain for "judicative logic."

And this entire method of proving non-contradiction seems to lead to a problem that is connected with ontology, namely with the theory of possibles (*possibilia*). Possibles are described as objects whose notes involve no mutual contradiction. Can this theory be applied to systems that according to axiomatics are free from contradiction, such as non-Euclidean geometry?

And in general to the problem of "mathematical existence," concerning which Aristotle and St. Thomas also have a theory that is yet to be developed?

* * *

From this brief survey we may conclude: in this part of philosophy, with great sorrow we see the absence of investigations by Scholastics. Without doubt this part of "judicative logic," which was so happily begun as a science by Aristotle, especially after the modern critique of these questions, must once again be cultivated. It is a matter of the most grave problems of the theory of knowledge, not only special but also general, of the problems of logic, and of epistemological problems regarding the structure and activity of science.

It is also a matter of completely constituting the foundations of geometry; but to this we shall not attend specifically. The primary problems will demand our attention, because there it is especially a matter of first principles, of a purely philosophical subject. We shall not need the considerations of higher mathematics; the mathematical elements will suffice.
