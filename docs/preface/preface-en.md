# PREFACE

This work deals with the "noetics of geometry"—that is, that branch of philosophical noetics whose proper object is the cognitive nature of the human mind insofar as it constructs geometric science.

The first part of this exposition (Chapters I–IV) represents a second, revised edition of a series of articles published several years ago in the journal *Gregorianum* [^1], incorporating certain adaptations deemed necessary to integrate them into the unified structure of the present volume. The remaining chapters (V–VII) contain the material already anticipated and promised in the concluding article (1943, p. 234), but whose development was hitherto prevented by wartime and post-war circumstances.

In these chapters, we examine from a noetic standpoint the axioms required for the construction of geometry—the so-called "foundations of geometry"—focusing strictly on the principal issues raised by modern critique. These, however, are examined thoroughly, for they possess paramount philosophical significance for all epistemological inquiries. Consequently, certain other axioms—such as those concerning the comparative measurement of lengths of lines with different curvatures—are not examined. In this respect, the axioms under examination constitute an incomplete system of indemonstrable or primitive propositions for the deduction of geometry, even within a classical deductive framework.

Yet we also possess a deeper and decisive reason for being content—indeed, for why we *must* be content—with an incomplete system of primitive, indemonstrable propositions. For, as we will demonstrate in Chapter V, establishing a complete system of this kind is impossible. Indeed, in the very process of deduction—not only in geometry, but in any science capable of continuous development—it proves necessary for the human mind repeatedly to invoke fresh primitive propositions that are indemonstrable yet known with necessity; and in those sciences, this is not only necessary, but continuously possible, *ad infinitum*. The noetic method of investigating these propositions is of paramount importance: it manifests the noetic nature of the human intellect, not only when examining the initial foundations of the sciences, but also in building them up deductively. Such repeated noetic exercise, especially through the intellectual habit (*habitus*) it engenders, is—if not strictly indispensable—immensely beneficial for general noetics, where it is commonly neglected even though conducting such inquiries is that discipline's proper office. Owing to this neglect, it is possible—and indeed frequently happens—that epistemological systems fall into grave error. Yet this does not happen inevitably; for the principles uncovered in such a noetic analysis are already implicitly present in any cultivated mind, naturally guiding its inquiries through the intellect. However, they are present only implicitly, or "in the exercised act" (*in actu exercito*); the proper task of noetics is to articulate these principles explicitly, elevating knowledge operative in the exercised act into fully "signified act" (*in actum signatum*).

We shall encounter this distinction frequently in what follows.

Moreover, in the title of this work we claim even more. We designate the noetics of geometry as the "origin" of the theory of knowledge. By this we intend to assert that epistemology not only *can* begin with the noetics of geometry (or at least with its principles), but that it *necessarily must* do so. We shall endeavor to establish this in the final section of Chapter VII, drawing both from the nature of the subject itself and from the doctrine of St. Thomas Aquinas—whose teaching on the judgment of the human mind we expounded at length in an earlier work, where we already made frequent reference to the noetics of geometry [^2].

It is historically evident that Scholastic cosmology cannot be successfully constructed, nor fully safeguarded from antinomies, without such inquiries—whether preliminary or concurrent. Furthermore, for other branches of philosophy as well, this continuous practical exercise upon which the human mind engages in this noetic analysis offers the greatest utility—not least for that part of metaphysics which examines the notion of actual existence (*esse*) itself. To illustrate this, we append to this volume our study, *On the Necessary Connections Between Existential Acts* (*De connexionibus necessariis inter actus existentiales*), published in *Gregorianum* (1953, pp. 603–639).

One further remark may be permitted here. There are philosophers—perhaps not a few—who, the moment they encounter geometric formulations or mathematical propositions in a philosophical treatise, even of the simplest kind, are immediately discouraged from reading and study. Let them have no fear: this modest effort will, if I am not mistaken, yield the richest fruits. Nothing more is demanded than what Plato himself is said to have required of any philosopher.

Rome, during the commemorative celebrations marking the completion of the fourth centenary since the founding of the Gregorian University, in the year 1953.

**P. HOENEN, S.J.**
