## 1. Overview and Core Philosophical Stakes

In Chapter 3, Father Petrus Hoenen begins his investigation of the second great foundational crisis of geometry: **the problem of exactitude** (*problema exactitudinis*). 

While elementary arithmetic deals with discrete, clearly bounded units, geometry claims absolute, infinite exactitude:
* Points without parts or magnitude (0D);
* Lines of zero breadth (1D);
* Surfaces of zero depth (2D);
* Perfect straightness, perfect circles, and exact points of intersection.

Yet all sensory experience and imaginative phantasms are irremediably **inexact**: drawn lines have physical thickness, points are specks of graphite or ink, and the visual field possesses an inescapable lower threshold of precision (*minimum visibile* / *limen exactitudinis*).

How does the human intellect cross this chasm?
* **Modern Formalists (J. Wellstein)** argued that this transition cannot be made by intellect; hence, the existence of points must be **postulated as an arbitrary act of will** (*ein reiner Akt des Willens*).
* **Empiricists (J. S. Mill)** denied exactitude altogether, declaring points without magnitude and lines without breadth to be "wholly inconceivable" fictions, reducing points to the *minimum visibile*.
* **Idealists (Proclus, Kant)** assumed that because exactitude cannot come from the senses, it must be recalled from a Platonic realm or imposed by subjective *a priori* forms of intuition.

Hoenen provides a profound Thomistic-Aristotelian solution: **exact indivisibles exist in potency in undivided extension, and are actualized by intellective division as boundaries**.

## 2. Section-by-Section Translation Notes & Philological Commentary

### § 1. On the Formulation of the Problem

#### 1. In What the Problem Consists
* **Double Challenge**: In Chapter 2 (Necessity), the problem concerned the *nexus* between concepts. In Chapter 3 (Exactitude), the problem concerns both the **necessary nexus** and the **objective reality of the concepts themselves**.

#### 2. Historical Perspectives
* **Aristotle’s Triangle Angle-Sum (*Post. Anal.* I, 31, 87b35)**:
  $$\text{καὶ εἰ ἦν αἰσθάνεσθαι τὸ τρίγωνον ὅτι δυσὶν ὀρθαῖς ἴσας ἔχει τὰς γωνίας, ἐζητοῦμεν ἂν ἀπόδειξιν}$$
  Aristotle points out that even if our eyes could see that a triangle's angles equal two right angles, sense could never establish this with mathematical exactitude; demonstration is required. St. Thomas adds that this is an example of truths that transcend sensory perception.
* **Proclus and Protagoras**: Proclus (*In Euclid. Elem.*, ed. Friedlein) cites the ancient sophist Protagoras, who argued that sensory straight lines never touch a circle at merely a single point, but along a small segment. Proclus used this sensory inexactitude to reject Aristotelian abstraction, embracing Platonic recollection.
* **John Stuart Mill’s Sensationalism**: In *A System of Logic* (1862, I, p. 255), Mill argues that exact geometric entities are physically impossible, mathematically fictitious, and inconceivable. Hoenen shows that Mill commits a category mistake: he confuses the **sensory phantasm** (which is indeed finite and thick) with the **intellective concept** (which grasps the exact boundary).
* **J. Wellstein and the Voluntaristic Postulate**: In the *Weber-Wellstein Enzyklopädie* (1925), Wellstein claims that arriving at a point by imagining a shrinking grain of sand (*Grenzprozeß*) plunges the mind into sensory darkness, forcing mathematicians to *postulate* the existence of points by a pure act of will. Hoenen agrees that shrinking grains of sand cannot reach a point, but demonstrates that the intellect arrives at points by an entirely different path: **the division of extension**.

### § 2. On the Primary Notions of Classical Geometry

#### 1. The Division of Extended Being and the Discovery of Indivisibles
Hoenen’s deductive derivation of indivisibles is one of the most brilliant passages in modern Scholastic literature:
1. **The Primary Datum**: We do not begin with an infinitesimal point; we begin with **extended being as extended** (*ens extensum ut extensum*)—a 3D solid body.
2. **Division**: The intellect intuits that extension is divisible. We divide a body into two parts.
3. **The Boundary (*Limes*)**: Between the two parts lies a boundary through which transition occurs.
4. **The Indivisibility of Transition**:
   * Along the parts (*iuxta partes*), the boundary is extended.
   * Between the parts (*inter partes*), **it is absolutely indivisible**!
   * *Proof by Reductio*: If the boundary between two parts possessed thickness, the body would be divided not into two parts, but into **three parts** (Part A, the boundary layer, and Part B); and for the boundary of that boundary layer the same regress would recur *ad infinitum*. Therefore, a genuine division into two parts strictly demands that the boundary between them be **indivisible in depth**!
5. **The Hierarchy of Indivisibles**:
   * The boundary of a 3D solid is a **surface** (2D), indivisible in depth.
   * The boundary of a 2D surface is a **line** (1D), indivisible in breadth.
   * The boundary of a 1D line is a **point** (0D), indivisible in all dimensions:
     $$\text{σημεῖόν ἐστιν οὗ μέρος οὐθέν} \quad \text{("A point is that which has no parts")}$$
6. **Aristotle's Definition**: A point is an **indivisible having position** (*indivisibile positionem habens*, Aristotle, *Post. Anal.* I, 27; *Metaph.* V, 6). Points are not nothingness; they are the termini of lines.

#### 2. The Möbius Strip Controversy
* **The Objection of Couturat and Wellstein**: Louis Couturat (1904) and J. Wellstein (1925) argued that a surface cannot be defined as the boundary of a solid, because unilateral surfaces (like the Möbius strip) have only one side and do not bound any solid.
* **Hoenen's Twofold Refutation**:
  1. *Local*: As Aurel Voss noted, any local element of a Möbius strip functions perfectly as an indivisible boundary separating two regions.
  2. *Global / Physical*: A physical Möbius strip made of paper has thickness; it is a **3D solid**! Hoenen discovers that this solid is bounded by **two distinct Möbius surfaces** (the wide twisted face and the edge-ribbon). Furthermore, when constructed with a thick rubber cross-section, the wide face visibly possesses two sides: one facing outer space, and one facing the interior of the rubber solid! The objection rests on an elementary oversight.

#### 3. General Epistemological Conclusions
* **Sensory Cognition as "Materia Causae"**: St. Thomas Aquinas (*S. Th.* I, q. 84, a. 6):
  > *"Sensory cognition is not the total and perfect cause of intellectual cognition, but rather in a certain manner the matter of the cause."*
* The intellect does not judge *of the phantasm* (which remains inexact), but *of the extended being in reality* through the phantasm.

#### 4. Infinite Divisibility and the Nature of the Continuum
* **A Continuum Cannot be Composed of Points**:
  * Points have zero extension ($0$ length).
  * Adding zeroes of length cannot yield extension:
    $$\sum 0 = 0$$
  * Hence, a line cannot be an actualized sum or collection of discrete points.
* **Aristotle vs. Cantor and Russell**:
  * Georg Cantor’s early set theory and Bertrand Russell viewed a line as an actualized infinite set of points. Hoenen shows this generates foundational antinomies.
  * In agreement with **L. E. J. Brouwer** and **Henri Poincaré**, a line is a **matrix and potentiality** in which points can be actualized, not an actualized infinity of discrete points.

#### 5. Poincaré and Nowhere-Differentiable Curves
* In *La valeur de la science* (1905), Poincaré argued that intuition is discredited because analysis discovered continuous functions lacking derivatives (such as Weierstrass curves and $y = x \sin(1/x)$ at $x = 0$), where no tangent exists.
* **Hoenen's Kinematic Refutation**:
  * Geometrically, continuous curves are generated by **continuous motion** ($x = t, y = f(t)$).
  * In $y = x \sin(1/x)$, to reach the origin $x = 0$, the generating point would have to execute an **actual infinity of oscillations in finite time**!
  * But by definition, an actual infinity cannot be traversed (*infinitum pertransiri nequit*).
  * Hence, the curve does not exist geometrically as a completed entity across $x = 0$. Anywhere away from zero, the curve exists and has a tangent. Intuition is completely vindicated.

#### 6. Geometry as a Constructive Science
* Geometric construction is neither passive empirical copying (Mill) nor an unconstrained free creation *ex nihilo* (Hilbertian formalism).
* It is the **intellective actualization of real potentialities in intelligible matter** (*materia intelligibilis*).

## 3. Key Greek, Latin, and German Technical Vocabulary

| Term | Language | Meaning & Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`σημεῖόν ἐστιν οὗ μέρος οὐθέν`** | Greek | Euclid's 1st definition: "A point is that which has no parts." |
| **`indivisibile positionem habens`** | Latin | Aristotle's definition of a point: an indivisible entity possessing spatial position (distinguishing it from the arithmetic unit, which lacks position). |
| **`limes`** | Latin | Boundary or limit; that through which transition from one part of an extension to another occurs. |
| **`inter partes` vs. `iuxta partes`** | Latin | Crucial distinction: between parts, the boundary is indivisible (0-depth); alongside parts, the boundary is extended. |
| **`materia causae`** | Latin | Thomistic formula (*S. Th.* I, q. 84, a. 6): sensible data provide the raw matter, but not the total cause, of intellectual knowledge. |
| **`Grenzprozeß`** | German | Limiting process; Wellstein's attempt to derive a point by imagining a shrinking grain of sand. |
| **`minimum visibile`** | Latin | The smallest sensible patch perceptible by vision; confused by J. S. Mill with the mathematical point. |
| **`infinitum pertransiri nequit`** | Latin | Classical Scholastic axiom: "The infinite cannot be traversed." Used by Hoenen to demonstrate why $y = x \sin(1/x)$ cannot reach the origin geometrically. |
| **`folium Moebii`** | Latin | The Möbius strip, used to prove both topological necessity (Ch. 2) and the boundary-character of unilateral surfaces (Ch. 3). |
| **`ens extensum ut extensum`** | Latin | Extended being precisely as extended; the proper object of geometry. |
