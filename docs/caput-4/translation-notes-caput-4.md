## 1. Scope and Strategic Position of Caput IV

Caput IV is the architectural heart of Father Hoenen’s epistemological treatise. Having established in Caput II that **necessity** is independent of empirical exactitude (refuting Kant’s identification of the two), and in Caput III that **exact indivisibles** (points, lines, surfaces) are boundaries (*limites*) of intelligible continuous extension, Hoenen now tackles the hardest problem of classical noetics:

> **How does the human intellect, operating upon inexact and imperfect sensitive phantasms, arrive at apodictically certain, mathematically exact figures (the straight line, the plane, the circle) and exact relations (congruence, parallelism, equality of angles)?**

Hoenen’s solution is revolutionary:
1. He identifies **extension as such** (*extensum ut est extensum*) as **intelligible matter** (*materia intelligibilis*), which serves as the **principle of individuation** for geometry.
2. He demonstrates that **motion/superposition** in Euclid (Prop. I.4) is epistemologically sound, refuting Bertrand Russell’s celebrated charge that it is a "logical juggle."
3. He establishes that **direction** is an irreducible primitive concept (*modus transeundi per punctum*), refuting Helmholtz’s charge of circularity and Wilhelm Killing’s critique of Leibniz.
4. He demonstrates that Euclidean geometry remains the unique, necessary geometry of uncurved extension, while showing that non-Euclidean geometries are genuine, non-contradictory mathematical structures representing curved submanifolds immersed in higher-dimensional Euclidean space.
5. He demonstrates that the problem of exactitude belongs **exclusively to the habit of first principles (*intellectus principiorum*)** and is completely absent from deductive science (*scientia conclusionum*), providing a Thomistic foundation for modern axiomatics.

## 2. Key Philological and Terminological Choices

### 2.1. *Principium Individuationis* and *Materia Intelligibilis*
- **Latin:** *Principium individuationis in geometricis*; *materia intelligibilis*.
- **Translation:** "Principle of individuation in geometric entities"; "intelligible matter."
- **Context & Scholastic Lineage:** In Thomistic metaphysics, primary matter (*materia prima*) signed by quantity (*materia quantitate signata* or *quantitas dimensiva*) is the principle of individuation for corporeal substances (*De Ente et Essentia*, c. 2; *ST* I, q. 50, a. 2). In geometry, substance is abstracted away, leaving quantity alone. Here, continuous extension as such acts as "intelligible matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* VII, 10, 1036a9–12: ὕλη νοητή; St. Thomas, *In De Trin.*, q. 5, a. 3, ad 3).
- **Noetic Function:** Because unformed extension is perfectly homogeneous and uniform, any specific shape (form) that can be realized in one region can be repeated identically in any other region. Extension provides both:
  1. The common ground allowing multiple individuals of the same lowest species (*species specialissima*); and
  2. The formal diversity of site (*diversitas situs*), which is the sole cause of numerical distinction between congruent figures.

### 2.2. *Situs*, *Positio*, and *Orientatio*
- **Latin:** *Situs*, *positio*, *orientatio*.
- **Translation:** "Site", "position", "orientation."
- **Distinction:** Hoenen introduces a precise three-way geometric distinction:
  - *Situs* (Site): The general Scholastic category of local determination (Aristotle's θέσις / *situs* as the 7th category).
  - *Positio* (Position): The translation coordinates of a figure in space—where its homologous points lie.
  - *Orientatio* (Orientation): The rotational aspect around a point—the direction of passage through that point.
  Two congruent figures can differ in *orientation alone* (rotated around a common pivot point), in *position alone* (translated with identical direction), or in both simultaneously.

### 2.3. *Passio Propria* / *Proprium*
- **Latin:** *Proprium*; *passio propria*.
- **Translation:** "Proper attribute"; "proper accident."
- **Aristotelian Context:** A *proprium* (ἴδιον) is a property that does not belong to the essential definition (*quid est*) of a subject, but flows necessarily and universally from its essence (Aristotle, *Topics* I, 5, 102a18; *Posterior Analytics* I, 4, 73a34–b5 on the fourth mode of *per se* predication, *quarto modo dicendi per se*). In Caput IV, Hoenen shows:
  - Mobility is a *proprium* of extended being as extended.
  - Direction is a *proprium* of linear magnitude.
  - Constant direction is the *proprium* of the straight line.

### 2.4. *Iudicia Virtualia* and *Syllogismi Virtuales*
- **Latin:** *Iudicium virtuale*; *syllogismus virtualis*.
- **Translation:** "Virtual judgment"; "virtual syllogism."
- **Epistemological Significance:** In medieval and modern Scholastic epistemology (*actus exercitus* vs. *actus signatus*), a virtual judgment is an intellectual intuition of a necessary connection that actively guides, motivates, and validates the mind’s progression, even though it is not formally enunciated as an explicit proposition. Hoenen extends this concept to *virtual syllogisms*: the mind joins two intuitions into an immediate actual affirmation without explicitly formulating a major and minor premise.

### 2.5. *Directio* as a Primitive Notion (*Modus Transeundi*)
- **Latin:** *Directio*; *modus transeundi per punctum*.
- **Translation:** "Direction"; "mode of passing through a point."
- **Noetic Significance:** Geometers often tried to eliminate "direction" or define it circularly via the straight line. Hoenen restores direction to its rightful status as a **primitive concept directly abstracted from the phantasm**: it is simply the orientation of passage through a point. Crucially:
  - Difference of length is a length (quantitative).
  - Difference of direction is **not** a direction; it is a qualitative deviation whose quantitative measure is an **angle**. Thus the angle is the quantitative measure of a qualitative cause (*mensura causae qualitativae*).

### 2.6. *Dignitates*, *Suppositiones*, and *Petitiones*
- **Latin:** *Dignitates* (axioms); *suppositiones* (hypotheses); *petitiones* (postulates).
- **Greek:** ἀξιώματα, ὑποθέσεις, αἰτήματα.
- **Source:** Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* I, 2 & 10 (76b23–28); St. Thomas, *In Post. Anal.* I, lect. 19.
- **Classification:**
  - *Dignitas* (Axiom): A principle that is not only true in itself, but must seem true to anyone (*necesse est videri quod per se sit verum*)—immediate, self-evident to the learner.
  - *Suppositio* (Hypothesis): A demonstrable proposition assumed by the teacher without proof, which is acceptable (*probabilis*) to the learner.
  - *Petitio* (Postulate): A proposition assumed without proof that the learner does not find immediately evident, which must be requested (*petitur*) from him.
  Hoenen proves that Euclid’s Postulates I–V are genuine *petitiones* (they require pre-geometric justification via noetic analysis of intelligible extension), whereas modern logicians mistakenly demand that they be self-evident *dignitates*.

### 2.7. *Termini Transcendentes*
- **Latin:** *Termini transcendentes*.
- **Translation:** "Transcendent terms" (formal/schematic symbols).
- **Source:** St. Albert the Great, *In Anal. Prior.* I, tract. I, cap. 9 (ed. Jammy, 1651, I, p. 298a); Pseudo-Thomas, *Summa totius logicae*, tract. VII, cap. 2 & 6.
- **Meaning:** In medieval logic, *termini transcendentes* refers to schematic letters ($A, B, C; S, M, P$) that "signify nothing and everything"—they abstract from all determinate material content and preserve purely formal consequence (*consequentia formalis*).

## 3. Major Philosophical Debates and Hoenen's Rebuttals

### 3.1. Bertrand Russell on Motion and Euclid I.4
In *Principles of Mathematics* (1903, §§ 390–391, pp. 405–407), Bertrand Russell launched a blistering attack on Euclid’s fourth proposition (the Side-Angle-Side congruence theorem):
> *"It has no logical validity, and strikes every intelligent child as a juggle... to speak of motion implies that our triangles are not spatial but material. For a point of space is a position, and can no more change its position than the leopard can change its spots."*

**Hoenen's Counter-Critique:**
1. **The Fallacy of Reified Empty Space:** Russell confuses *ens reale extensum* (real extended entity, whether physical body or intelligible continuous quantity) with the Newtonian/Kantian *spatium imaginarium* (an empty, static container of points). Points are not rigid pegs nailed down in absolute nothingness; they are boundaries of extended parts.
2. **Mobility as an Essential Proprium:** Mobility is a strict property of extension as such. A figure in a body is mobile because the body is mobile; in pure geometry, translation represents the passive capacity of homogeneous extension to receive the identical form in another site.
3. **Superposition Eliminated Statically:** Hoenen delivers a devastating technical checkmate: Euclid’s theorem can be proved **entirely without motion**! Using the principle of individuation, from point $D$, direction $DE$, angle $D$, and side $DF$, a unique triangle $DEF$ is determined. By the principle of individuation, a triangle congruent to $ABC$ exists in that position; since only one triangle can be constructed from those data, $DEF$ is identically congruent to $ABC$. Q.E.D.
4. **Philological Vindication of Euclid:** Euclid’s Greek terms (ἐφαρμοζομένου, τιθεμένου) do not require physical dragging, but can be read as the static actuation of congruent elements in the new site.

### 3.2. Henri Poincaré on Rigid Bodies and Geometry
In *La Science et l'Hypothèse* (p. 80), Henri Poincaré famously asserted:
> *"Si donc il n'y avait pas de corps solides dans la nature, il n'y aurait pas de géométrie."* ("If, therefore, there were no rigid bodies in nature, there would be no geometry.")

**Hoenen's Counter-Critique:**
Poincaré reduces geometric necessity to empirical physics. Geometry does not require physically inelastic atoms or solid iron rods; it requires only the **passive intelligibility of continuous extension**, which remains identical across all regions. Even in a universe composed entirely of viscous fluids or gases, the intelligible matter of extension would retain the capacity to receive congruent figures.

### 3.3. Hermann von Helmholtz and Wilhelm Killing on Direction
- **Helmholtz’s Dilemma:** In his *Schriften zur Erkenntnistheorie* (1921, p. 141), Helmholtz claimed that defining the straight line by direction is a vicious circle, because direction can only be defined by the straight line.
- **Killing’s Dilemma:** In *Einführung in die Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1893, I, § 3), Wilhelm Killing argued that Leibniz’s attempt to define parallels by equal direction relative to a transversal presupposes the entire parallel postulate, because one cannot know whether lines making equal angles with one transversal make equal angles with all transversals.

**Hoenen's Resolution:**
1. Direction is not defined by the straight line; it is abstracted directly from any line passing through a point (as its infinitesimal tangent/mode of passage).
2. The straight line is defined as a line of **constant direction** (*directio constans*).
3. The plane is defined as a surface of constant direction (homogeneous in all directions).
4. Because the plane is the principle of individuation, the angular deviation between directions is uniform throughout the entire plane. Therefore, equal corresponding angles along one transversal necessarily entail equal corresponding angles along **every** transversal. Killing's objection falls away once extension is recognized as intelligible matter.

### 3.4. Felix Hausdorff and Non-Euclidean Geometry
Felix Hausdorff asserted in 1904 (*Das Raumproblem*, p. 3) that mathematics can dismiss unexamined (*ungeprüft ad acta legen*) any philosophical attempt to deduce Euclidean space as a thought-necessity.

**Hoenen's Masterful Epistemological Clarification:**
Hoenen does not commit any of the classical blunders:
1. He does **not** attempt to prove Postulate V from the other axioms alone (which is mathematically impossible).
2. He does **not** claim Postulate V is immediately self-evident (citing Felix Klein, he shows that as the angle sum approaches $180^\circ$, the intersection point recedes to infinity and escapes sensitive intuition).
3. He does **not** substitute a pseudo-axiom (such as Clavius’s equidistant line or Wallis’s similar triangles), which suffers from the exact same defect of empirical inexactitude.

Instead, Hoenen establishes:
- Non-Euclidean geometry is completely valid as a formal deductive system and as the geometry of **intrinsically curved submanifolds** (e.g. Beltrami's pseudosphere) immersed in higher Euclidean space.
- But Euclidean geometry is the **unique geometry of uncurved extension as such**. The non-Euclidean geometries use the words "straight line" and "plane" equivocally for geodesics and curved surfaces. Humanity was never deceived: classical geometry rightly asserted the necessity of Euclidean relations for *genuinely straight lines and flat planes*.

### 3.5. Bertrand Russell on Euclid I.1 (The Equilateral Triangle)
In *Principles of Mathematics* (pp. 404–405), Russell charged that Euclid’s very first proposition fails logically because Euclid assumes without evidence that the two circles drawn on base $AB$ intersect at point $C$.

**Hoenen's Epistemological Vindicating Analysis:**
1. Russell demands a direct intellectual abstraction of an exact equilateral triangle from a sensory phantasm. But such direct abstraction is **critically impossible**, because the senses cannot distinguish an equilateral triangle from one whose sides differ by $0.0001\%$.
2. Euclid solves the problem of exactitude by an **indirect construction**: describing two circles with radius $AB$ guarantees by definition that $AC = AB$ and $BC = BA$.
3. The intersection of the two circles is an immediate intuitive connection of continuous extension (*Verknüpfung*). The intellect grasps that if the distance between centers ($AB = r$) is less than $2r$, the continuous circumferences must intersect.
4. Russell's formalist critique blinds him to the actual cognitive mechanism (*le cheminement de la pensée*) by which the intellect transcends sensible inexactitude.

## 4. The Epistemological Partition: *Intellectus* vs. *Scientia*

In § 7, Hoenen articulates one of his most profound epistemological breakthroughs:

| Epistemological Domain | Scholastic Faculty / Habit | Cognitive Mechanism | Relation to Exactitude |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Foundations of Axioms** | *Intellectus Principiorum* (Habit of First Principles) | Abstraction from the phantasm; intellectual intuition of intelligible matter (*resolutio ad sensum*). | **100% of the Problem of Exactitude resides here.** The mind must transcend sensory fuzziness to grasp exact boundaries and constant direction. |
| **Deductive Geometry** | *Scientia Stricte Dicta* (Habit of Conclusions) | Formal syllogism; symbolic deduction (*consequentia formalis*); preservation of connection via middle term. | **0% of the Problem of Exactitude resides here.** Syllogistic form is inherently exact (like arithmetic). Once exact premises are given, exactitude transfers automatically to conclusions. |

This explains why **modern mathematical axiomatics** succeeded so brilliantly at formalization while failing completely at epistemology:
- Hilbert and the formalists restricted mathematics entirely to *scientia stricte dicta* (pure formal deduction from uninterpreted symbols).
- In doing so, they severed mathematics from *intellectus principiorum* (the grounding of axioms in intelligible reality), creating the false impression that geometry is a purely arbitrary game of uninterpreted tokens.
- Hoenen restores the Thomistic bridge: formal axiomatics is valid as deductive technique, but true geometry is grounded in the noetics of intelligible extension.
