# Translation Notes & Philological Commentary: Caput IV

**Work:** Petrus Hoenen, S.J., *De Noetica Geometriae: Eiusque Cum Rebus Necessitate et Exactitudine* (Rome: Gregorian University Press, 1954)  
**Chapter:** Caput IV: *De Problemate Exactitudinis. Pars II: De Figuris et Relationibus Exactis* (pp. 95–156)  
**Focus:** Philological Analyses, Historical Context, and Scholarly Apparatus  

---

## 1. Philological and Terminological Analyses

### 1.1. *Principium Individuationis* and *Materia Intelligibilis*
- **Latin:** *Principium individuationis in geometricis*; *materia intelligibilis*.
- **Translation:** "Principle of individuation in geometric entities"; "intelligible matter."
- **Context & Scholastic Lineage:** In Thomistic metaphysics, primary matter (*materia prima*) signed by quantity (*materia quantitate signata* or *quantitas dimensiva*) is the principle of individuation for corporeal substances (*De Ente et Essentia*, c. 2; *ST* I, q. 50, a. 2). In geometry, substance is abstracted away, leaving quantity alone. Here, continuous extension as such acts as "intelligible matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* VII, 10, 1036a9–12: ὕλη νοητή; St. Thomas, *In De Trin.*, q. 5, a. 3, ad 3).
- **Noetic Function:** Because unformed extension is perfectly homogeneous and uniform, any specific shape (form) that can be realized in one region can be repeated identically in any other region. Extension provides both:
  1. The common ground allowing multiple individuals of the same lowest species (*species specialissima*); and
  2. The formal diversity of site (*diversitas situs*), which is the sole cause of numerical distinction between congruent figures.

### 1.2. *Situs*, *Positio*, and *Orientatio*
- **Latin:** *Situs*, *positio*, *orientatio*.
- **Translation:** "Site", "position", "orientation."
- **Distinction:** Hoenen introduces a precise three-way geometric distinction:
  - *Situs* (Site): The general Scholastic category of local determination (Aristotle's θέσις / *situs* as the 7th category).
  - *Positio* (Position): The translation coordinates of a figure in space—where its homologous points lie.
  - *Orientatio* (Orientation): The rotational aspect around a point—the direction of passage through that point.
  Two congruent figures can differ in *orientation alone* (rotated around a common pivot point), in *position alone* (translated with identical direction), or in both simultaneously.

### 1.3. *Passio Propria* / *Proprium*
- **Latin:** *Proprium*; *passio propria*.
- **Translation:** "Proper attribute"; "proper accident."
- **Aristotelian Context:** A *proprium* (ἴδιον) is a property that does not belong to the essential definition (*quid est*) of a subject, but flows necessarily and universally from its essence (Aristotle, *Topics* I, 5, 102a18; *Posterior Analytics* I, 4, 73a34–b5 on the fourth mode of *per se* predication, *quarto modo dicendi per se*). In Caput IV, Hoenen shows:
  - Mobility is a *proprium* of extended being as extended.
  - Direction is a *proprium* of linear magnitude.
  - Constant direction is the *proprium* of the straight line.

### 1.4. *Iudicia Virtualia* and *Syllogismi Virtuales*
- **Latin:** *Iudicium virtuale*; *syllogismus virtualis*.
- **Translation:** "Virtual judgment"; "virtual syllogism."
- **Epistemological Significance:** In medieval and modern Scholastic epistemology (*actus exercitus* vs. *actus signatus*), a virtual judgment is an intellectual intuition of a necessary connection that actively guides, motivates, and validates the mind’s progression, even though it is not formally enunciated as an explicit proposition. Hoenen extends this concept to *virtual syllogisms*: the mind joins two intuitions into an immediate actual affirmation without explicitly formulating a major and minor premise.

### 1.5. *Directio* as a Primitive Notion (*Modus Transeundi*)
- **Latin:** *Directio*; *modus transeundi per punctum*.
- **Translation:** "Direction"; "mode of passing through a point."
- **Noetic Significance:** Geometers often tried to eliminate "direction" or define it circularly via the straight line. Hoenen restores direction to its rightful status as a **primitive concept directly abstracted from the phantasm**: it is simply the orientation of passage through a point. Crucially:
  - Difference of length is a length (quantitative).
  - Difference of direction is **not** a direction; it is a qualitative deviation whose quantitative measure is an **angle**. Thus the angle is the quantitative measure of a qualitative cause (*mensura causae qualitativae*).

### 1.6. *Dignitates*, *Suppositiones*, and *Petitiones*
- **Latin:** *Dignitates* (axioms); *suppositiones* (hypotheses); *petitiones* (postulates).
- **Greek:** ἀξιώματα, ὑποθέσεις, αἰτήματα.
- **Source:** Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* I, 2 & 10 (76b23–28); St. Thomas, *In Post. Anal.* I, lect. 19.
- **Classification:**
  - *Dignitas* (Axiom): A principle that is not only true in itself, but must seem true to anyone (*necesse est videri quod per se sit verum*)—immediate, self-evident to the learner.
  - *Suppositio* (Hypothesis): A demonstrable proposition assumed by the teacher without proof, which is acceptable (*probabilis*) to the learner.
  - *Petitio* (Postulate): A proposition assumed without proof that the learner does not find immediately evident, which must be requested (*petitur*) from him.
  Hoenen proves that Euclid’s Postulates I–V are genuine *petitiones* (they require pre-geometric justification via noetic analysis of intelligible extension), whereas modern logicians mistakenly demand that they be self-evident *dignitates*.

### 1.7. *Termini Transcendentes*
- **Latin:** *Termini transcendentes*.
- **Translation:** "Transcendent terms" (formal/schematic symbols).
- **Source:** St. Albert the Great, *In Anal. Prior.* I, tract. I, cap. 9 (ed. Jammy, 1651, I, p. 298a); Pseudo-Thomas, *Summa totius logicae*, tract. VII, cap. 2 & 6.
- **Meaning:** In medieval logic, *termini transcendentes* refers to schematic letters ($A, B, C; S, M, P$) that "signify nothing and everything"—they abstract from all determinate material content and preserve purely formal consequence (*consequentia formalis*).

## 2. Historical, Intertextual & Bibliographical Context

### 2.1. Bertrand Russell on Motion and Euclid I.4
- **Source:** Bertrand Russell, *Principles of Mathematics* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1903, §§ 390–391, pp. 405–407).
- **The Charge:** Russell attacked Euclid’s fourth proposition (Side-Angle-Side congruence):
  > *"It has no logical validity, and strikes every intelligent child as a juggle... to speak of motion implies that our triangles are not spatial but material. For a point of space is a position, and can no more change its position than the leopard can change its spots."*
- **Hoenen's Philological & Epistemological Counter-Critique:**
  1. *The Fallacy of Reified Space:* Russell confuses *ens reale extensum* with Newtonian *spatium imaginarium* (an empty, static container). Points are not rigid pegs in nothingness; they are boundaries of extended parts.
  2. *Mobility as a Proprium:* Mobility is a strict property of extension as such. Translation in geometry represents the capacity of homogeneous extension to receive the identical form in another site.
  3. *Static Determination:* Hoenen proves Euclid’s theorem entirely without physical motion: by the principle of individuation, from point $D$, direction $DE$, angle $D$, and side $DF$, a unique triangle $DEF$ is determined identically congruent to $ABC$.
  4. *Euclid's Greek Vocabulary:* Euclid’s terms (ἐφαρμοζομένου, τιθεμένου) do not require physical dragging, but denote the static instantiation of congruent elements in the new site.

### 2.2. Henri Poincaré on Rigid Bodies and Geometry
- **Source:** Henri Poincaré, *La Science et l'Hypothèse* (Paris: Flammarion, 1902, p. 80).
- **The Thesis:** *"Si donc il n'y avait pas de corps solides dans la nature, il n'y aurait pas de géométrie."* ("If, therefore, there were no rigid bodies in nature, there would be no geometry.")
- **Hoenen's Counter-Critique:** Poincaré reduces geometric necessity to empirical physics. Geometry does not require physically inelastic solids or steel rods; it requires only the *passive intelligibility of continuous extension*, which remains identical across all regions. Even in a universe composed entirely of viscous fluids or gases, the intelligible matter of extension would retain the capacity to receive congruent figures.

### 2.3. Hermann von Helmholtz and Wilhelm Killing on Direction
- **Sources:** 
  - Hermann von Helmholtz, *Schriften zur Erkenntnistheorie* (ed. Hertz and Schlick, Berlin: Springer, 1921, p. 141).
  - Wilhelm Killing, *Einführung in die Grundlagen der Geometrie* (Paderborn: Schöningh, 1893, I, § 3).
- **The Objections:**
  - *Helmholtz:* Claimed that defining the straight line by direction is a vicious circle, because direction can only be defined by the straight line.
  - *Killing:* Argued that Leibniz’s definition of parallels by equal direction relative to a transversal presupposes the parallel postulate, because one cannot know whether lines making equal angles with one transversal make equal angles with all transversals.
- **Hoenen's Resolution:** Direction is abstracted directly from any line passing through a point as its infinitesimal mode of passage. The straight line is defined as a line of *constant direction* (*directio constans*). Because the plane is the principle of individuation, the angular deviation between directions is uniform throughout the entire plane, resolving Killing's objection.

### 2.4. Felix Hausdorff and Non-Euclidean Geometry
- **Source:** Felix Hausdorff, *Das Raumproblem* (Leipzig, 1904, p. 3).
- **The Assertion:** Hausdorff asserted that mathematics can dismiss unexamined (*ungeprüft ad acta legen*) any philosophical attempt to deduce Euclidean space as a thought-necessity.
- **Hoenen's Contextual Clarification:** Hoenen does not attempt to prove Postulate V from the other axioms alone, nor does he claim it is an immediately self-evident *dignitas*. Instead, he establishes that Euclidean geometry is the unique geometry of *uncurved extension as such*, while non-Euclidean geometries are valid formal systems representing intrinsically curved submanifolds (such as Beltrami's pseudosphere) immersed in higher Euclidean space.

### 2.5. Bertrand Russell on Euclid I.1 (The Equilateral Triangle)
- **Source:** Bertrand Russell, *Principles of Mathematics* (1903, pp. 404–405).
- **The Critique:** Russell charged that Euclid’s first proposition fails logically because Euclid assumes without proof that two circles drawn on base $AB$ intersect at point $C$.
- **Hoenen's Epistemological Vindicating Analysis:** Euclid solves the problem of exactitude by an *indirect construction*: describing two circles with radius $AB$ guarantees by definition that $AC = AB$ and $BC = BA$. The intersection of the two circles is an immediate intuitive connection of continuous extension (*Verknüpfung*): because the distance between centers is less than $2r$, the continuous circumferences must intersect.
