# ON THE PROBLEM OF EXACTITUDE

## PART II. ON EXACT FIGURES AND RELATIONS

### Preliminary Remarks

In the preceding chapters we dealt with the two principal problems: necessity and exactitude. Of this problem of exactitude, only the first part was considered by us—namely that part which, as was said there (Chapter III), does not require the consideration of extension, such as the "principle of individuation" in mathematical entities. The second part of this problem, whose solution depends upon this principle, now comes to be considered.

Let us briefly recall why the problem of geometric exactitude arises; let it be permitted to repeat here what was said earlier (Chapter III, § 1, pp. 65–69 ff.):

> "What geometry asserts concerning its objects—points, lines, surfaces, their mutual relations, the shapes of figures, e.g., the straightness of a line, the equality or proportions of figures—all these are considered as absolutely exact. Sensitive data, however, from which classical geometry derives its origin, do not possess this exactitude. Neither sense nor imagination can perceive points and lines, but only bodies extended in three (or at least two) dimensions. They cannot distinguish lines that are slightly curved from straight ones; consequently, they can never decide whether a given line is straight. They can perceive equality between two lines or surfaces approximately, but not exactly; the same must be said of proportions. Things that are very distant cannot be perceived, and yet geometry deals with extended entities that extend into infinity.
>
> If, therefore, geometry in its origin depends upon sensitive data, how can this transition to perfect exactitude be explained; or is it perhaps merely an illusion?"

Regarding the existence of objects corresponding to the exact notions of point, line, and surface, we examined the question in the third chapter and vindicated the classical notions; we elucidated the transition from sensitive data to perfectly exact intellective notions. We can therefore safely use these notions in what follows; we know how the intellect, in abstracting these exact notions from the inexact phantasm, can transcend sensitive data; we know that this operation is critically justified. It remains for us to examine other questions. Now, as before, we are looking more to noetic questions pertaining to the theory of knowledge than to those that must be examined to fully expound the foundations of geometry; for purely noetic questions are most worthy of being considered in themselves. Therefore, this inquiry too intends to be purely philosophical, noetic, and not mathematical.

* * *

We must diligently examine the processes that take place in the human mind when it intends to construct elementary geometry from its first principles. There may lie hidden there operations of the intellect—operations that are simultaneously abstractive (for they take their origin from the phantasm) and intuitive; for by a simple intuition of the mind we can intuit and affirm both the necessity of certain connections and their exactitude. We say these operations "lie hidden" there—not as though they were unconscious, for on the contrary they are cognitive and intuitive; nor as though they were without influence upon subsequent judgments, for indeed they determine these judgments; but because their objects are not explicitly affirmed, much less enunciated by means of propositions. We are dealing here with *virtual judgments*, which we already encountered earlier (Chapter II, § 2, no. 6; § 3, no. 3; Chapter III, § 2, no. 5 A); there it was also said that at times—or often—it is difficult to accurately detect, explicitly affirm, and enunciate these virtual judgments. The necessary connections affirmed in these judgments, if formulated explicitly, are certainly known intuitively in virtual judgments, and in the mental process these intuitions exert an influence. Thus (in the passage cited) we found that two intuitive cognitions concerning the "proper attributes" (*passiones propriae*) of multitudes always precede the simple cognition and affirmation of what results from the addition of two numbers. Such prior virtual judgments are also found in examining the problem of geometric exactitude.[^1]

And in order to understand well the character and mode of cognition of the human mind in general, careful attention must be paid to what we found earlier: The function of these prior judgments is not like the office of a major premise—that is, of a universal principle—in a syllogism; for there it was demonstrated that the movement of which virtual judgments form a part is not syllogistic; it is not the deduction of a conclusion from those judgments; the movement that leads to the universal affirmation of these judgments is precisely the inverse of the syllogism. These things must be attended to if we wish to understand accurately the mode of operation of the human mind. For such virtual judgments usually precede, as intellective intuitions of necessary relations, those judgments that affirm other relations subsequent to those prior ones—namely, those judgments that are customarily enunciated as classical axioms. The task of the philosopher, as was said before (Chapter I, § 1), is to examine those operations of the human mind from which the cognition of classical axioms flows forth. This must not only be attended to in order to examine the foundations of geometry, but must also be considered accurately in examining the noetic nature of the human mind itself in general. St. Thomas says (*Quaestiones Disputatae de Veritate*, q. 10, a. 6, *sed contra* 2):

> "All our cognition originally consists in the knowledge of the first indemonstrable principles. The cognition of these in us arises from sense, as is clear at the end of the *Posterior Analytics*. Therefore our science arises from sense."

We are speaking of all our cognition considered in its origin, "originally"; the principles, however, in which this origin is said to consist, are indemonstrable principles that regard the "proper object" of the human mind—namely, the form that is in matter; and the first principles of this are above all geometric principles, for these are first and best known to us. In order to have insight into the mode of operation of our mind, we must institute a "resolution" (*resolutio*) to first principles; and therefore (*De Veritate*, q. 12, a. 3, ad 2): "Because the first principle of our cognition is sense, it is necessary to resolve in some way (*quodammodo*) to sense all things concerning which we judge." St. Thomas says that "all things concerning which we judge" must be resolved to sense; but he adds "in some way" (*quodammodo*); in order that this "mode" may become known to us, the best, if not the only, object of examination will be the consideration of those things from which the intuition of the first principles of geometry arises. This intuition transcends the sensitive data from which it arises in two respects: 1° with respect to necessity (and consequently universality); 2° with respect to exactitude.

* * *

In this second part of our inquiry into the problem of exactitude, cases therefore occur that deal with the perfect equality (congruence) of two or more figures; with the existence of perfect lines, i.e., of a perfectly straight line, of a circle whose radii are perfectly equal; with the existence of a perfect plane; with the perfect equality of two proportions; with the perfect concurrence of three straight lines in a single point, etc. Judgments concerning such cases certainly cannot be directly formed through abstraction from the inspection of a phantasm or sensitive datum; in all these cases, figures can be given that are in reality inexact, but which approach so close to exactitude that the imperfection cannot be apprehended by the senses. Geometry, nevertheless, deals with certainty concerning perfectly exact relations of figures. We must inquire whether a principle is given from which this certitude legitimately arises. We shall see that this principle is given, and that it is the cognition of extension as it is the principle of individuation for mathematical entities. Moreover, it will be clear that this principle is indeed everywhere employed as a virtual judgment.

## § 1. ON THE PRINCIPLE OF INDIVIDUATION IN GEOMETRIC ENTITIES

We are not dealing here with the metaphysical principle of individuation and the celebrated metaphysical question raised regarding that principle; we are dealing only with geometric entities as they are geometric; but what we shall say undoubtedly coheres with the metaphysical question.

Here, therefore, we speak only of geometric entities—that is, of figures that are the boundaries (*limites*) of an extended entity, of lines and surfaces, and of the parts of extension that can be circumscribed by these boundaries; figures can differ and agree both in quantity and in shape (*forma*).

Can multiple figures be given that agree exactly both in quantity and in shape, and therefore differ by number alone? Geometry answers affirmatively by considering what is called the *congruence* of figures and establishing theses regarding that congruence.

What is the foundation of this affirmation? It cannot be sensitive experience. Sensitive experience, to be sure, induces the human mind to conceive the idea of congruent figures. Two circles, two triangles, or any other figures can be observed between which the senses can perceive no difference regarding quantity and shape. But since we know that the senses cannot arrive at perfect exactitude, it is not permissible on that account to affirm that these two figures are exactly congruent; indeed, it is not even permissible on that account to say that two such figures can be given—that is, that they exist mathematically. But our intellect, in this case too, can transcend sense and affirm the possibility, the mathematical existence, of congruent figures; and indeed it can do so in the same way as was done in the preceding case—namely, in the case of indivisibles, which cannot be perceived by sense: by understanding the nature of extension as it is extended, and the nature of a certain proper attribute that flows from it. For considering any figure whatever, the boundary of extension, our mind understands that this bounded extension, by its very nature, can be a part of a greater extension, in such a way that other parts of this extension, as they are extended, are of perfectly the same nature among themselves and with the first part; the parts of extension, as it is extended, are perfectly homogeneous, uniform (that is, unformed, not determined by a form) with the whole and with each of the other parts. Whence it immediately follows: if any figure (determination) is possible in one part, in any other part a figure specifically and perfectly the same will be possible; and indeed in such a way that its site (*situs*), and its site alone, differs in a continuous manner from the site of the prior figure. Or thus: alongside every figure, another figure is possible that differs by site alone, and that "alongside" (*iuxta*) is to be understood in such a way that it can be continuously varied.

Extension as such is therefore truly "intelligible matter" (*materia intelligibilis*) for geometric entities; from this matter, specifically identical figures can be actuated in different parts. And thus extension contains the twofold element required for a principle of individuation:

1. First, because it implies the distinction of parts of perfectly the same nature, by that very fact it brings with it the possibility of multiplying geometric figures in the same lowest species (*species specialissima*), with respect to their quantity and their shape. This is the first element of the principle of individuation.
2. But secondly, it likewise implies the other element—namely, that in which those forms differ among themselves. For it necessarily brings with it a possible diversity of site (*situs*). These figures, specifically the same, therefore differ by site alone, by position alone in extension, by number alone.

All these things are clear and are immediately understood to flow from the understood nature of extension as it is extended. They are understood by a virtual judgment, even if they are never enunciated; and this judgment is always associated with our concept of extension as such. Extension so conceived we shall indicate in what follows (as is customary) by the word "space"; we certainly do not intend to speak of imaginary space, the receptacle of bodies, which is non-being (*non-ens*); we intend to speak of a real extended entity, but only as it is extended; and in this sense it can be applied to imaginary space, which is conceived as extended after the manner of a real entity; it is a receptacle because it is extended.

In passing, we note: from this consideration it follows that extension as such is without doubt *a certain* principle of individuation (for geometric entities); it does not follow that it is the *sole* principle of individuation; hence the metaphysical question is not yet resolved by these considerations.

At first sight, a certain difficulty could be raised, for instance from the consideration of an ellipsoidal figure. On such a surface, a figure traced in one part cannot be traced in any other part, because the "curvature" of this surface is not everywhere the same. We do not think this difficulty ought to detain us long. This surface itself is already not an extension determined by no form; it is not pure extension. Moreover, the extension thus bounded by this surface is, or can be, part of a greater extension; and in any part of this, a figure congruent to the first can be repeated.

Congruent figures differ by site alone. We observe that the slightest difference in site already brings with it a numerically different figure. Let us take a simple example in which all that we were saying is immediately understood from the inspection of the phantasm. We have a certain straight line, let us say of one meter. From this extension there can be cut out—in this intelligible matter there can be actuated—figures that are specifically determined thus: they are half of the given line, i.e., lines of half a meter. Infinitely many lines of this species can be actuated from the given line by division; not, of course, simultaneously. Simultaneously there can be actuated, by division at the accurately middle point, the right half and the left half. But these are not the only ones. For a line that begins at a point at distance $1/3$ from the start of the line, and ends at a point situated at distance $5/6$, is half of the line; it is an individual of this species. And thus it is clear, on account of the infinite divisibility of the line, that infinitely many half-lines can be actuated; not, of course, simultaneously; each is of the same species, they differ among themselves purely numerically, and this difference depends upon the sole diversity of site in which they arise; and the slightest diversity of site is the cause of numerical difference.[^2]

In this entire exposition, special attention must be paid to this: the understanding of extension as it is the principle of individuation does not arise from the fact that we perceive by sense sensibly congruent figures; such a perception can indeed be the occasion from which we conceive the notion of congruence; but that exact congruence is possible cannot be justified from that perception alone; the transition from this perception to the affirmation of congruence would be illegitimate; indeed, the transition to the affirmation of congruence realized in this case remains illegitimate from that perception alone. In order that the transition to the affirmation of the possibility of congruence be legitimate, an intellective intuition flowing from the understanding of the nature of extension is required and suffices—an intuition that recognizes extension as the principle of individuation in geometric entities. And this intuition was without any doubt naturally present in the human mind while it constructed classical geometry, just as the intuition of points, lines, and surfaces was present, through which it already transcended sensitive cognition; they were present as virtual judgments.

From what has been said so far, however, we cannot yet know which exact figures are possible—whether perfectly straight lines, perfect circles, etc., exist mathematically. What is established so far is only this: if they are possible in one part of extension, they are also possible in another. Yet this already suffices for us to deduce various geometric theses regarding exact congruence, as we shall immediately show by an example.

First, however, another matter must be considered. Geometers often employ the "motion" of figures to prove such theses; Euclid already did so in the first thesis he demonstrates on this subject. Because difficulties are raised against this mode of procedure, and in general against the use of the notion of "motion" in geometry, this notion, which intimately coheres with the nature of extension as the principle of individuation, must first be elucidated.

## § 2. ON MOTION IN GEOMETRIC ENTITIES

Euclid employs the motion of figures in the first theorem he demonstrates concerning the congruence of figures (Book I, Theorem 4; ed. Heiberg, pp. 16–19); so at least it is usually understood by commentators, although, as we shall see, another interpretation is possible. There he proves the congruence of two triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$ ($DEZ$) that have two sides equal ($AB = DE$ and $AC = DF$) and the angles between them, $A$ and $D$, also equal; the possibility of these data already depends upon the principle of individuation. To demonstrate the thesis, he transfers, they say, the first triangle and applies it to the second: $A$ to $D$, $AB$ to $DE$; by that very fact, on account of the data, $B$ will fall on $E$, $AC$ will be applied to $DF$, and the point $C$ to $F$. Because between $E$ and $F$ there exists only a single straight line (for "two straight lines cannot enclose a space"), $BC$ will coincide with $EF$; and hence the whole triangles themselves coincide.

Euclid therefore in his argument applies translation—motion, therefore—of figures; which he does very rarely (besides this place, I, 8 and III, 24 are cited); certain moderns, however, vehemently carp at this method. We shall see immediately that this method can be avoided so that the structure of the argument is scarcely changed; nevertheless, its legitimacy must be entirely sustained. No one carps at Euclid more vehemently than Bertrand Russell:[^3]

> Euclid's argument "has no logical validity, and strikes every intelligent child as a juggle."[^4]

Our experience, however, seems to testify that Euclid's argument is no less convincing to intelligent children than any other, in this or any other subject. But let us hear the first reason that Russell adduces. He says:

> "In the first place, to speak of motion implies that our triangles are not spatial but material. For a point of space is a position, and can no more change its position than the leopard can change its spots ... motion, in the ordinary sense, is only possible to matter, not to space."[^5]

The other reasons especially regard the notion of a "rigid body"—that is, of a figure invariable under translation—and will immediately be considered by us, also according to other modern authors. This first reason is the most fundamental, because it regards the very notion of "motion in geometric entities"; and therefore it must be more carefully examined.

Russell says, therefore, that motion belongs to bodies, not to space, which is considered by geometry, whose points are positions that themselves cannot be moved. But Russell errs; geometry is indeed the science of extension as it is extended (not, for instance, as it is colored); this they often express by saying: geometry is the science of "space"; which can be admitted if the word "space" here signifies: extension as such; but it is false if the word is taken for "imaginary space," which is a fictitious receptacle of bodies. Geometry certainly holds validly of that imaginary entity, but only because it is conceived as extended; geometry, however, also holds validly of a real body as it is extended. A figure realized in such a body can be moved, just as the body itself, because extended, is mobile according to place.

These things must be strongly urged, because the possibility of motion is a *proprium*, in the strict sense, of extension as it is extended; motion would not be possible in a purely spiritual world; the possibility of motion is immediately understood to flow from extension as it is extended. And indeed extension is required under a twofold respect:
1. First, that which is moved must be extended; at least that which is moved *per se*; things that are joined with an extended entity can be moved *per accidens*, according to the excellent theory of Aristotle.
2. Next: in order that motion be possible, another extension is required (place, namely) with respect to which the mobile is moved.

If these two are really present, then and then only is the possibility of motion present. And because these things are understood to flow from extension itself as such, extension—the object of geometry—is not fully understood unless the possibility of motion is understood, even of a figure, which is the boundary of a part of extension. The cognition of this proper attribute is again naturally present in the human mind as a virtual judgment that is always associated with the concept of extension. But the notion of motion does not pertain to geometry under every aspect involved in it; in part it is the object of metaphysics and physics; and therefore different aspects must be distinguished.

Let us distinguish beforehand motion itself from that which results from motion: the *new position* of the figure. And it must be noted that Euclid's argument considers only the new position resulting from motion, not the motion itself in itself; and this would already suffice to justify the argument. More, however, must be said: even motion itself, according to certain of its aspects, pertains to the field of pure geometry; and here we must distinguish carefully.

In motion, its velocity and its cause can be considered. The former does not pertain to geometry insofar as this velocity is a certain *intensity*. This intensity indeed, like every intensity (cf. the author's *Cosmologia*, chapter "De mensura qualitatum," 4th ed., pp. 184–203, 518–527; *Filosofia della natura inorganica*, pp. 176–186), can be "measured," and indeed through purely quantitative effects; for it is measured by the length of the line that the mobile traverses in a certain time (in a unit of time). But the numbers resulting from that measurement are not purely cardinal numbers; the quantity that is thus measured is not predicamental quantity, but "virtual quantity" (*quantitas virtutis*), intensity. And so considered, motion does not pertain to geometry, but to physics or natural philosophy. Immediately, however, in velocity itself we shall detect one aspect that is geometric; which is not to be wondered at, for it is an intensity in a geometric subject.

Next: because motion is a change, it requires an efficient cause; under this aspect too it is a notion alien to geometry, pertaining to metaphysics and physics. Hence this aspect is completely neglected in geometry.

There are, however, two aspects in motion itself according to which it pertains to geometry, so that this science of extended being would be imperfect if they were omitted. For we are dealing with two "proper attributes" of extension as such—namely, with the prerequisite conditions that motion may be possible:

1. The first condition was already partly described above: in order that motion be possible, it is required that both the mobile and that according to which it is moved—I mean place—be extended; from extension as such, therefore, flows the possibility of motion. But now something must be added: for it is clear that for a figure, or a figured body, to be moved without any intrinsic change, the extension alongside which, or rather in which, it is moved—whether this be considered purely geometrically (as a coordinate system) or be a real place, such as the ether—must in each of the positions successively occupied by the mobile be capable of receiving a figure congruent with the figure to be moved, differing by position alone. And it must be noted that this must be possible in a continuous manner. But this property of extension, which is prerequired for the possibility of motion of an invariable figure, and from which this possibility flows, is what was called above the "homogeneity or uniformity of extension as it is extended"; it is that on account of which extension as such is the principle of individuation. Because we naturally understand this proper attribute of extension, therefore, and therefore alone, do we naturally understand the possibility of motion of an invariable figure; it is present in our mind as a virtual judgment. This property, however, belongs to extension as such, and therefore the consideration of motion according to this prerequisite condition pertains to pure geometry.[^6]

2. The other aspect according to which it pertains to geometry is this: motion essentially has **direction**. Let us consider the motion of a point along a determinate line $AB$. The motion from $A$ to $B$ is really distinct from the inverse motion from $B$ to $A$. Moreover: that point can move in a plane, let us say through point $P$, along various lines that can be drawn through that point; and in each case the motions are diverse. This diversity results from a purely geometric datum: from the diverse direction of lines in the plane relative to one another. This notion of "direction" cannot be neglected if we wish fully to understand extension as such; moreover, it pertains to the very first notions we have of extension. Therefore, this notion must be examined more diligently; we shall do so later; for now let us attend to two things.

First: direction is something qualitative; it is not indeed intensive, nor is it purely quantitative. This is immediately evident from the fact that the difference of two predicamental quantities is a quantity of the same genus: the difference of two lengths is a length. But the difference of two directions is not a direction. It pertains indeed to quantity; for from that difference there immediately results something quantitative, which can later be developed so that we arrive at the notion of an *angle*; and angles can be added to and subtracted from one another; the difference of angles is an angle. Another thing I should like to observe now: velocity also has "direction"; it is not a pure intensity, but is what in physics is called a **vector**.

Aristotle, in his treatise on time (*Physics* IV, 11, 219a10–18), already accurately explains that the direction of time, together with its continuity, arises from the direction of motion, and—what pertains to our subject—this arises from direction (position, θέσις) and continuity in magnitude. He says:

> ἐπεὶ δὲ τὸ κινούμενον κινεῖται ἔκ τινος εἴς τι καὶ πᾶν μέγεθος συνεχές, ἀκολουθεῖ τῷ μεγέθει ἡ κίνησις· διὰ γὰρ τὸ τὸ μέγεθος εἶναι συνεχὲς καὶ ἡ κίνησίς ἐστιν συνεχής ...
> 
> ("Since that which is moved moves from something to something, and every magnitude is continuous, motion follows magnitude; for because magnitude is continuous, motion also is continuous...")

First, therefore, continuity is found in extension, and thence it follows in motion. But the order of parts as well—the order of prior and posterior, which was indicated above by the words "from something to something" (ἔκ τινος εἴς τι)—has the same origin. He continues:

> τὸ δὴ πρότερον καὶ ὕστερον ἐν τόπῳ πρῶτόν ἐστιν. ἐνταῦθα μὲν δὲ τῇ θέσει· ἐπεὶ δ' ἐν τῷ μεγέθει ἔστι τὸ πρότερον καὶ ὕστερον, ἀνάγκη καὶ ἐν κινήσει εἶναι τὸ πρότερον καὶ ὕστερον, ἀνάλογον τοῖς ἐκεῖ
> 
> ("The prior and posterior, then, is first in place. There indeed it is by position [θέσει]; but since in magnitude there is prior and posterior, it is necessary that in motion also there be prior and posterior, analogous to those there.")[^7]

These are the things we were describing above; because time is treated here, attention is paid to the direction of a single dimension, of a single line alone.

Hence it seems we must say: to consider motion does not pertain to geometry insofar as it implies velocity, except insofar as velocity involves "direction"; for to understand this, the consideration of extension as it is extended is required, which is the foundation of the notion of direction. It does not pertain to geometry to deal with the efficient cause of motion itself. But purely geometric is the consideration of the possibility of continuous motion, of the possibility of motion of an invariable figure, of the possibility of directions of diverse motions.

Hence it is no wonder that among geometers motion is employed as a means of demonstration, indeed as a means of definition. Well known is the definition of a line as that which is caused (or described) by a point that moves; thus from the motion of a line arises a surface, from the motion of a surface a solid. These can be admitted as descriptions, not as definitions strictly so called; for we would fall into the difficulties we set forth earlier (Chapter III, § 1, no. 2). Already in the time of Aristotle these descriptions were in use. He says (*De Anima* I, 4, 409a3–5): 

> φασι κινηθεῖσαν γραμμὴν ἐπίπεδον ποιεῖν, στιγμὴν δὲ γραμμήν
> 
> ("They say that a line moved makes a plane, and a point [moved makes] a line.")[^8]

St. Thomas also knows this method of describing lines, surfaces, and solids, but he ascribes it to the imagination of geometers.[^9]

That this method can be useful for defining special lines we already saw earlier (Chapter III, § 2, no. 6 B). And it must be noted: "time," which was introduced there as a "variable," is in the end "eliminated" from the formulas; what remains is purely geometric; but it depends upon the velocity of the motions that are "compounded."

The consideration of motion, therefore, without any doubt pertains to pure geometry; geometry explains the foundation and conditions of this notion. If extension is examined in its nature and simultaneously in this proper attribute by which it is the principle of individuation, it is evident that it grounds the possibility of motion of an invariable body, and also grounds the notion of constant direction and of those things that follow thence. Considering these things, we shall arrive at the axioms regarding the congruence of figures and regarding exact figures. Both points we shall investigate in what follows.

## § 3. ON THE CONGRUENCE OF FIGURES

Euclid, therefore, by employing motion as a means of demonstrating congruence, does not exceed the limits of geometry, which considers extension as extended. Nor can the manner in which he does this be imputed to him as a fault, although this is sometimes done by those who do not properly distinguish physical elements from purely geometric elements in motion. And on account of this, those difficulties pertain to our investigation, which is entirely engaged in examining the cognition of the human mind regarding the foundations of geometry.

The same Bertrand Russell mentioned above mixes these diverse elements and thence takes occasion for critique (loc. cit.). I pass over for now the fact that he also intimates that a body transferred to a place where the temperature is different is by that very fact changed in its dimensions. Another objection, however, can appear to be of greater moment.

He says: bodies that are moved so as to be applied to one another are supposed to be *rigid bodies*; and this is affirmed by not a few others. But, they add, in the nature of things no absolutely rigid bodies are given.

This objection would be entirely valid if the possibility of a congruent figure—perfectly and exactly equal to the first, namely to that which must be moved—had to be known by us just as physical laws must become known: namely, from sensitive observation alone, aided, if the case requires, by measuring instruments. In such a case the intellect does nothing except render in concepts and experimental judgments what was observed. But in our case the matter is not so, and this diversity between our case and that of a physical law must be carefully noted.

To be sure, experience, which teaches that sensibly rigid bodies exist—bodies, therefore, that move in such a way that their extension and shape remain sensibly the same—can already suggest the notion of the motion of a body that remains invariable in quantity and shape while moving. But if we had nothing more, we would be engaged in a cognition that does not surpass experience either with respect to necessity or with respect to exactitude. But our geometric cognition de facto surpasses it. And the reason is also evident. That experience can be the occasion used by the intuition of our intellect, but its data are not the cause of our total intellective cognition; they are rather the "matter of the cause" (*materia causae*). And this in our case can easily be detected.

The reason for affirming the possibility of motion of a body and of a geometric figure that are not changed in motion is understood by us—perhaps on the occasion of such experience—to be: the proper attribute of the extension in which they move to receive in each of its parts, which continuously succeed one another, such a figure as was present in another part; and that necessarily, and that exactly. Nothing else must be supposed, specifically nothing concerning the physical properties of bodies—concerning the question whether the efficient cause of motion deforms the shape of the mobile, whether it resists a small, large, or all force, whether it is rigid. The possibility of that motion is understood to be present on the part of the extension both of the mobile itself and of the "space" in which it is to be moved; we are dealing with a *passive possibility*.

Hence whoever has clear insight into the origin of this intellective intuition, which sees the possibility of motion of an invariable figure flowing from the very nature of extension, must deny the assertion made by the distinguished Poincaré: *"If, therefore, there were no rigid bodies in nature, there would be no geometry."*[^10] We understand, nevertheless, that even a non-rigid body can at least be moved without its extension and shape being changed, provided suitable forces are present. Nor do these words "provided suitable forces are present" imply a restriction; for only the passive part of motion has weight here; and this part is entirely intelligible to us precisely because of the understood nature of extension. The thesis that Poincaré posits completely equates our geometric intuition with the origin of purely experimental cognition; which, by the witness of our intuition itself, is false on this point as well.

The manner in which Euclid and others use the motion of an invariable figure in their demonstration is justified in every respect from the foundation that is presupposed: namely, from the proper attribute of extension by which it is the principle of individuation; from the fact that it is intelligible matter from which figures can be made that differ by site alone, and therefore by number alone.

Hence the question can arise whether, employing this foundation alone, without the intermediary of the translation of a figure, the same thesis can be demonstrated. And this indeed seems to be the case.

For the Euclidean demonstration can be constructed without the notion of translation in this way:
- From point $D$ (of triangle $DEF$) there is given one and only one straight line in direction $DE$; on it, along the same direction toward $E$, there is given one point, and one only, namely $E$, such that segment $DE$ is equal to side $AB$.
- On that side of this line facing line $DF$, there is given one line, and one only, that makes an angle ($D$) equal to angle $A$, namely the line $DF$ itself.
- On this line there is given again one point, and one only (namely $F$), such that segment $DF$ is equal to side $AC$.
- There is given again one straight line, and one only, that joins $E$ and $F$.
- Triangle $DEF$ is therefore the unique triangle that can be constructed in the position determined by elements $DE$, $D$, and $DF$, which are respectively equal to elements $AB$, $A$, and $AC$.
- Now we apply the property of extension expressed by the principle of individuation: namely, in every position there is given a triangle that differs by number alone from triangle $ABC$—that is, which is congruent to it.
- Because, however, from the given elements $DE$, $D$, and $DF$ only a single triangle is composed, namely $DEF$, this triangle will be congruent to triangle $ABC$. Q.E.D.

Every single element we have used is found in Euclid's demonstration; yet no translation has been employed; the theorem follows from the principle of individuation, which expresses the proper attribute (*passio propria*) of extension. And this principle seems always to be employed in similar reasoning as a virtual judgment. That by means of this principle—whose intuition is always and naturally blended with the operations of our mind—we are able to transcend the inexactitude of sensibility appears in the course of the demonstration. Because, however, it is not explicitly affirmed, not even mentally, it must be said to be present there as a virtual judgment, which is detected by analysis and passes into a formal judgment. Other intuitions are also present: for example, those on account of which we affirmed that only a single point, a single line, a single segment is present. And therefore the analysis of such a demonstration is useful and necessary for detecting the mode in which the human mind operates in geometric matters, and therefore for constituting the doctrine concerning our geometric cognition.

After what we have seen regarding this demonstration of Euclid, perhaps we should advance further and say: Euclid himself in his demonstration did not intend to apply the translation of a figure (triangle), but only to employ the principle of individuation, as we did. We know, of course, that historians do not so interpret Euclid; to us, however, it seems that his exposition admits this interpretation. For where he speaks of the "application" of triangle $ABC$ to $DEF$ (ἐφαρμοζομένου τοῦ τριγώνου) and of the "placing" of point $A$ on $D$ (τιθεμένου τοῦ σημείου), we can understand his words not as transferring triangle $ABC$, but as constructing anew, actuating from its elements in a new site ($DEF$), a triangle congruent to it, which according to the principle of individuation exists in every position; and the possibility of actuating the individual elements is again established from the same principle. This interpretation seems to be possible; if it is true, then even in Euclid's demonstration translation is not employed as a means of demonstration. And again it appears that the translation of an invariable figure as a means of demonstration is equivalent to its foundation, which is expressed by the principle of individuation.

We can conclude: just as in abstracting the exact notions of surface, line, and point (as indivisibles either under a certain respect or simply) from inexact sensitive data, our mind, from the understood nature of extension, not only conceives exact notions, but understands those objects to exist mathematically (Chapter III, § 2, no. 2); so also in the case of congruence, from understood extension as the principle of individuation, it not only conceives exactly equal figures differing by site alone, but understands them to exist, and can logically deduce other truths from them.

And what we saw earlier (Chapter III, § 2, no. 7) can be repeated for this case: it is manifest that we do not (freely) create these objects; we do not will congruent figures to exist, but we detect them as possible in extension, which is the intelligible matter from which they can be made.

The principle of individuation, as it has been applied so far, whether with translation or without it, can only demonstrate *relative* exactitude. For it proves only that, from the fact that a figure present in one site can also be actuated in any other, two figures that agree in certain elements also agree in the others. Supposing that a triangle is given whose sides are $AB$ and $AC$, between which is angle $A$, another triangle is possible such that $DE = AB$, $D = A$, $DF = AC$; and it is proved that these two triangles also agree with each other in the other elements. But that triangle $ABC$ is possible, in which line $AB$ is exactly straight and so forth, is not proved here but supposed; but this being supposed, exact congruence is proved. Hence, if the principle of individuation is employed only thus, only relative or hypothetical exactitude is demonstrated. But the principle of individuation can again be employed to overcome the inexactitude of sensibility in demonstrating the possibility, the mathematical existence, of *absolutely exact figures*, such as the straight line. Concerning which we must now treat.

## § 4. ON EXACT FIGURES

### 1. Preliminary Remarks

Already in the second chapter, in "inexact geometry," we were able to speak of points, lines, and surfaces—inexact, of course—and to enunciate proper relations concerning these elements: points are situated on a line and on a surface, lines are situated on a surface; and conversely: a line passes through a point, a surface passes through a line. Concerning points constructed on the same line, proper relations manifest themselves to our mind, expressed by the word "between"; in similar fashion, a line can be situated between others constructed on the same surface. These notions and these proper attributes, as we saw in Chapter III, are preserved in exact geometry, which discovers true surfaces, true lines, and true indivisible points; and there they lead to propositions verified only in it, e.g.: between two points infinitely many others can be constructed (Chapter III, § 2, no. 6). The axioms pertaining to this part constitute two series: axioms of connection (*Verknüpfung*) and axioms of order (*Anordnung*).

These notions and these axioms are also preserved in Hilbert, although what the meaning of these words is in his work cannot be asked. For the meaning is to be drawn from intuition, which Hilbert indeed admits as a heuristic means, but which he then wishes to exclude, to the end that he may then proceed purely logically, through pure syllogisms, independently of all geometric intuition (Chapter I, § 5). His terms and axioms are therefore pure symbols, just as in minor logic, where the moods and figures of syllogisms are treated, symbols are introduced for subject and predicate. But it must be carefully noted: Hilbert indeed admits that the natural meaning of numbers is known and employed; moreover, notions such as this are supposed known: "an operation that can be repeated into infinity"; and perhaps others; e.g., in § 3 he begins to speak of a segment (*Strecke*), and to introduce it he uses the expression "system of two points" (*das System beider Punkte*), where the word "system" seems to be supposed known.

Hilbert indeed follows intuition in selecting ("creating") notions and axioms, and remains closer to classical geometry than others, but he does not wish to use intuition as a foundation.[^11] But in these matters three remarks seem to be of some or even great importance:

1. First: he enunciates as mere notions, not as axioms, those things that express the "proper attributes" of which we spoke; this he does in his "declarations" (*Erklärungen*). These, however, are true judgments; e.g., the possible relations between points and lines are expressed by the words: "points are situated on a line," and so forth. But here a judgment is present, at least a virtual one, which expresses that these relations are proper attributes (*passiones propriae*) of these objects, flowing from their nature. Those propositions that are then explicitly enunciated as axioms are nothing but specifications of these relations. In pure axiomatics, whose principal intention is explicitly to enunciate all axioms, these ought not to have been missing as axioms. And this is a confirmation of what we often said: namely, that there are virtual judgments in our mind that are not affirmed there because we immediately proceed to inquire into further things. They certainly have an influence upon the affirmation of what we subsequently discover.

2. The second remark is this: because we understand these proper attributes flowing from extension in the strict sense—for the intelligible is being—extension with its proper attributes is known as being, and these relations as real; and we can proceed in our investigation, considering extension in the classical manner as intelligible and real.

3. The third remark is: relations such as "point $A$ is situated on line $a$" and "line $a$ passes through point $A$" are considered as entirely the same (or purely inverse); which does not seem to be true in every respect. For this second geometric relation implicitly seems to say more, at least in such a way that it demands further determination; for different lines pass through the same point in different ways. And this further determination seems to be of great importance, because it leads to the elaboration of an intuitive notion of great moment, which seems almost universally neglected, indeed at times despised, by geometers in the beginnings of geometry: to the notion of **direction**, of which mention was already made above. Concerning this and concerning those things that cohere with it, we must now speak more broadly: first, as it is found in sensitive data; next, whether, like other notions we already dealt with, it can pass into exact geometry. With this notion cohere the notions of straight and curved line, and of plane and curved surface.

### 2. On the Notion of Direction

We were saying: the expressions "point is situated on a line" and "line passes through a point" do not say the same thing in every respect, because this second formula implicitly says more and demands a further determination that is not demanded in the first formula. Behold what we intend.

If only a single line is considered, as was already said above, on which point $P$ is situated, the two expressions can appear equivalent. Yet even here two directions on the line can be distinguished, which are opposite or inverse relative to each other. This direction coheres with the notion of possible motion through this point along this line. The mental actuation of motion teaches that two motions are possible that are really distinct from each other. Our mind intuitively recognizes this possibility as flowing from the nature of the line, an extension of one dimension. But simultaneously it intuitively recognizes this possibility as flowing from the nature of the line, from a proper attribute of this static extension—namely, from the fact that this line has these two opposite directions according to the order, the position (θέσις), of its parts. If we say only: "the point is situated on this line," we do not attend to this; if we express the inverse relation: "the line passes through the point," we can already attend to these opposite aspects, inverse in direction.

These matters are of greater moment if we attend not to a single dimension, the line, but consider it as situated in an extension of two or three dimensions: a surface or a solid ("space"). This will lead to the notion of "direction relative to that surrounding extension."

Let two lines $a$ and $b$ pass through the same point $P$; this says more than the expression: "$P$ is situated on $a$ and on $b$"; for the passage is different if $a$ is considered, and if $b$ is considered. Even if $a$ and $b$ differ in no other respect—if, therefore, they are congruent according to both directions on the lines themselves—they nevertheless differ in the way they pass through $P$; they differ in the direction of their passage; otherwise they would not be two. They differ in their passage through $P$, no longer as in the case of a single line, in which opposite directions are to be distinguished, but relative to the surrounding extension in which $P$ is situated; this extension requires at least two dimensions. The meaning of these words "mode of passage" or "direction of passage," and the "existence" of those modes, is immediately clear to anyone inspecting a simple figure of two small segments of a line passing through the same point, which do not coincide. It is manifest: the expression "$a$ and $b$ pass through $P$" demands a further determination that specifies the mode of passage more precisely.

It is necessary, therefore, to introduce the notion of the direction of lines, also relative to each other in an extension of at least two dimensions, and to examine it more accurately; for it is found in the first geometric data by anyone who pays attention, among the first proper attributes of extension, and is understood to flow from the nature of extension; the notion of direction cannot be suppressed; nor does it emerge only after a long evolution of geometry. That this notion is also realized in exact geometry, insofar as it considers lines without breadth, true lines, is immediately clear; under other respects it can also be developed to justified exactitude, as we shall immediately see; first, certain proper attributes of this notion must be examined.

The diversity of directions of lines relative to each other at the point $P$ through which they pass implies a divergence or deviation of one relative to the other in the extension in which $P$ is situated; this must be distinguished from direction itself, as an effect from its cause. This divergence must now be examined more accurately.
- Let there be, in the same surface, a third line $c$ that passes through the same point $P$; this, like $b$, has a divergence relative to $a$. It is immediately evident: this divergence can be greater or less than that which $b$ has, in this sense: it begins to contain a greater or lesser part of the surface near point $P$.
- Another proper attribute is: a deviation can be given, relative to $a$, in one sense or in the other; the senses can be distinguished, for instance, by the words "to the right" and "to the left," or by other words that suppose a similar opposition relative to each other.
- Suppose $c$ has a greater divergence relative to $a$ than $b$; the divergence that $c$ has can be considered as the sum of two divergences: $b$ relative to $a$, and $c$ relative to $b$; divergences can be added and subtracted; (this no longer holds if the three lines are not situated in the same surface).

Another proper attribute is very curious: divergence in one sense can become greater (in the opposite sense it then becomes less), but it cannot grow beyond all limits; for in the end the divergence becomes zero; the directions of the two lines at or through point $P$ are the same. Thus this deviation of direction, which admits of measure, cannot grow into infinity like extension itself and many figures in it; therefore, the addition of divergences cannot be repeated into infinity, at least in the proper sense.

This notion of direction, therefore, and the notion of its divergence—which later will lead to the exact notion of an angle—truly pertain to first notions, and their proper attributes to things that are immediately manifest. All these things are read in the phantasm as necessary, flowing from the nature of lines situated in a surface; without the intuition of the phantasm they cannot be fully understood, neither with respect to the meaning of the notions nor with respect to the judgments that attribute mathematical existence to these notions and affirm their proper attributes as necessary; these judgments again are present as virtual judgments in the natural mode of procedure of the human mind, and are elaborated only by philosophical analysis. They are again "constructions" whose intelligible matter is given—namely, extension as such.

These things flow from what was said in § 3: namely, the possibility of motion of a rigid body (i.e., one that is not intrinsically changed by motion alone) supposes that the surrounding extension (this is "place") is the principle of individuation, and therefore permits the congruence of figures in a continuous manner. These figures can then be moved so that they perfectly coincide. This can be applied to congruent lines passing through the same point of the surrounding extension. In this case a special motion can be considered: that which consists in the revolution of a line, which is rotated in such a way that this same point of the line always remains fixed. These things, which we know from experience as sensibly verified, are also known, from the consideration of extension as it is the principle of individuation, as exactly possible, as flowing from the "homogeneity" of extension as such. These lines whose revolution is considered here can be both sensibly curved and sensibly straight (and, after establishing the existence of exactly straight lines, also such); these sensible lines are already naturally known by man.

And note moreover: congruent figures differ "by site alone" in the surrounding extension. Here, in the case of the revolution of a rigid line around a fixed point, we detect a new case of "difference in site alone," which is found only in lines (and compounds of lines), not in points. This difference is the difference of **direction in passing through the same point**. We detect this immediately, intuitively, after establishing the possibility of congruence as above.

Two congruent lines can therefore differ from each other "by site alone" in a twofold manner:
1. One mode is that which has just been described: two homologous points (i.e., points that divide lines into parts that can be called, for example, left and right parts, in such a way that the two right parts and the two left parts of the two lines are congruent to each other) on both lines remain coincident with the same point of the surrounding extension, but the passage of the lines through this point is diverse.
2. They can moreover differ in such a way that these homologous points coincide with diverse points of the surrounding extension. After introducing the comparison of directions through distant points (no. 3), we understand that in the second case it can happen that these two lines have specifically the same direction through the points of the surrounding extension occupied by the two homologous points of the lines. Then the lines can be said to differ "by position alone," but to have the same "orientation."

If congruent figures are composed of many lines, these things can be applied to homologous lines in them. A twofold diversity "in site alone" is also found in these figures: one, where two homologous points coincide with the same point of the surrounding extension, can be called diversity in **orientation alone**; the other will be diversity in **position**; in the general case of two congruent figures, both diversities are present simultaneously.

### 3. On the Continuum of Directions

Divergence, which according to what was said above admits of measure, can be so small as to escape the senses. The same must be said of the diversity of directions; the directions of passages through a single point can be continuously varied; hence around any point whatever there is given an entire continuum of directions, a continuous sheaf (*fascis continuus*), whose apex is the point itself. But on that account too, in considering divergence and its measure, certain things can be found that pose anew the problem of exactitude. That a continuum of directions is given at individual points, however, seems established from what has been said.

It is also immediately understood without further examination that around any point whatever of a surface there can be given only a continuum of directions that is already found in the extension of three dimensions in which the surface is the boundary of parts; not all of this is found in the surface, but only a section of it; those directions that pass through this boundary—the surface, namely—are not found in the surface, but the rest are, at least at the very point $P$ being considered. Hence it is that at individual points of three-dimensional extension, three-dimensional continua of directions are given; in surfaces, only two-dimensional ones. (It must be added: it can happen that at one point of a surface a different continuum of directions from the three-dimensional continuum is preserved than at another point, as will immediately be more fully expounded).

For continuity must also be considered in this problem in another way. We began our analysis by considering two small segments of lines (even sensibly curved ones) that, intersecting at point $P$, pass through that point according to diverse directions. These segments can be taken smaller, for they are divisible. If previously segments $PA$ and $PB$ were considered, now $PC$ and $PD$ can be taken, in which $C$ is between $P$ and $A$, and $D$ between $P$ and $B$; and this process can be repeated, and that into infinity, and that on both sides of the line passing through $P$. Proceeding thus, however, we now approach a more exact intellective consideration of the direction of lines at point $P$—that is, of the mode in which they pass through that point. In order to have an entirely exact consideration, whose possibility is intuitively manifest to our mind, we must consider the **limit**—that is, the mode in which the mobile points $C$ and $D$, on lines $PA$ and $PB$, coincide with $P$. And this "diversity in the mode of coinciding" for mobile points is the true and exact diversity of directions of both lines at point $P$, of the mode in which they pass through that point.

This direction is considered in the infinitesimal calculus, and there it is evident that even a straight line and a curved line can pass through in the same way, i.e., according to the same direction. Nor is it surprising that the same mode of procedure is employed where velocity of a mobile passing through point $P$ is treated (which is considered in mechanics), and where contact between diverse curves or determining the direction of the tangent to a curve is treated; not surprising, we say, that there the same mode of procedure—differentiation or an equivalent mode—is employed; for the direction of motion or velocity, as it is a vector, has its origin from the geometric direction of the line.

The accurate notion of direction in infinitesimals directs our attention to another notion, which we can thus subject to exact analysis: two lines can pass through the same point $P$ in the same way, according to the same direction (thus diverse curves and a straight line that have a point of contact at $P$); at $P$, or through $P$, they have the same direction, but then at other points they will have diverse directions. This notion then invites making a twofold further step: to compare the directions of lines at diverse points, and also the parts of a single line with one another.

### 4. Curves and Straight Lines

In inexact geometry mention can already be made of curved and straight "lines" that are known sensibly; as is manifest, we now intend to speak only of sensibly curved and sensibly straight lines. But this distinction must also be sustained if we pass to lines worthy of the name, which lack breadth; they will be exact lines, but their straightness or curvature is taken in a sense still inexact, as sensibly known. But to these lines we can apply the elements of exactitude that we recently discovered.

To accomplish this, let us recall that the directions of lines with respect to their likeness or diversity are also understood of lines as they pass through diverse, distant points, even in sensible geometry. Segments of lines distant from each other that are traced on a surface, such as this page, can be compared under the respect of direction. In one part let there be a segment that tends from the left side toward the right; in another, a segment drawn from the bottom upward. These directions are without doubt diverse. But two segments can also be given, on the right and left sides, that both tend upward in (almost) the same way; and intermediate cases are given.

Let us look again at the first case, in which the directions are certainly diverse: on one line let there be point $P$, on the other point $Q$. We already know that through $P$ there passes an entire continuum of directions, so that lines can be given that, beginning from the first, are continuously varied according to direction, until finally the direction opposite to the first is attained. Among these directions only one can be present (if one is present, which must be examined) that is *exactly similar* to the direction of the other line through $Q$. This direction will be perfectly and exactly similar; it will be specifically the same. Concerning the notion, this is already established; not yet concerning mathematical existence.

What we were saying about diverse lines can be applied to distinct parts of the same line. These too, relative to direction, can be compared with one another, and will have diverse directions or specifically the same, perfectly similar direction. We can use these to define the notions of straight and curved line:
- A line will be **straight** if the direction according to which it passes through one point is preserved further in it, specifically the same—if through any subsequent or preceding point it continues to pass in the same direction, without deviation.
- If at one point there is an abrupt change of direction, it will be a "broken line."
- If the direction is continuously varied, it will be a **curved line**.

That such lines, only sensibly exact, exist, experience teaches; whether exact lines mathematically exist must be investigated; for thus in this case the problem of exactitude returns. Concerning the notions, it is now established; concerning mathematical existence, not yet; or as Aristotle speaks: it is established concerning the *quid est* ("what it is"), but not yet concerning the *an est* ("whether it is"); and in a question concerning a proper attribute (*proprium*), as is the question of "straightness" and "curvature," it must first be established concerning the *quid est*.

We remark in conclusion that the mode of procedure we have followed to establish these exact notions and to pose the problem of their existence seems in every respect to correspond to the natural process of the human mind, *le cheminement de la pensée*; we were instituting nothing other than an analysis of this mode of procedure, beginning from sensitive data.

This also seems to be the notion that Euclid intended in his definition of a straight line, concerning which there has been so much dispute; this at least seems to us to be very probable, for on account of the obscurity of the words it can scarcely be established with certainty concerning his intention. His definition, however, is this (Book I, Definition 4, *Elements*, ed. Heiberg, I, pp. 2–3):

> εὐθεῖα γραμμή ἐστιν, ἥτις ἐξ ἴσου τοῖς ἐφ' ἑαυτῆς σημείοις κεῖται.
> 
> ("A straight line is that which lies evenly with the points on itself.")

The phrase "to lie evenly" (*ex aequo iacere*, ἐξ ἴσου κεῖσθαι) seems truly to admit this interpretation: "to pass through the points in the same way, i.e., according to the same direction, without deviation."

Geminus also, in Proclus (*In Primum Euclidis Elementorum Librum Commentarii*, ed. Friedlein, p. 185), from the straight line understood either as a property or as a definition, infers that from any point to any point a single straight line can be drawn; he says:

> ἑπόμενόν ἐστι τῷ ῥύσιν εἶναι τοῦ σημείου τὴν γραμμὴν καὶ τὴν εὐθεῖαν ὁμαλὴν καὶ ἀπαρέγκλιτον ῥύσιν
> 
> ("It is consequent upon a line being the flux of a point, that a straight line is an even and undeflected flux.")

### 5. On the Surface

Lines sensibly straight and curved are therefore given, and concerning their definition by means of the primitive notion of direction, even if they are supposed exact, it is now established; we must further investigate whether lines that are exactly straight exist mathematically.

Similarly must it be said of surfaces. From sensitive data it immediately becomes known that surfaces exist that are curved—which have a concave face and an opposite convex face. Surfaces are also given that, sensibly at least, are neither concave nor convex, but plane. On curved surfaces sensibly straight lines can sometimes be drawn (this will also remain true in exact geometry), but never through every point and according to every direction. Nor is a straight line joining two arbitrary points of the surface situated entirely in the surface, but on the concave side it passes outside it.

A sensibly plane surface given in our experience is such that through any point whatever there passes an entire continuum (sheaf) of sensibly straight lines that lie entirely in the surface; every straight line joining two arbitrary points of the surface is situated entirely in it; this surface is concave on neither side. These connections, of course, are verified only sensibly.[^12]

Whether in exact geometry the straight line, the plane, and their connections, according to what we briefly touched upon, truly exist mathematically must now be inquired. In other words: we must now investigate whether in these cases, which also involve qualities of figures, the transition from the imperfection of sensibility to the exactitude of the intellect can be justified from a means that permits the transition.

Meanwhile, however, it must again be observed: in the natural process of the human mind according to which geometry was constructed, virtual judgments can be found such that they already contain this justification. If in this problem too, as in the preceding ones, the matter stands thus, the mind already in that natural process immediately from sensitive data, verified only sensibly, legitimately deduced exact geometry, without having explicitly affirmed those judgments. Then the task of the philosopher is to inquire into, consider, and explicitly enunciate these virtual judgments; this task will perhaps be difficult not in itself, but only on account of historical circumstances.

The principal things to be investigated, as follows from what was said above, are roughly these:
- Whether in distant lines that can have a sensibly similar direction, this likeness can exist *exactly*;
- Whether in extension constant direction—and consequently[^13] the straight line that sensibly exists—is also given *exactly*;
- Whether to sensibly plane surfaces there also correspond *exact planes*, in which every exact straight line whose two points are situated in them is contained entirely in them;
- Whether the plane is such that around each of its points there are found continua of directions that differ among themselves by number alone.

Above it was said that the definition of a straight line posited by Euclid not without probability signifies its constant direction. Similarly it is not improbable, as it seems to us, that the proper attributes of a plane surface discovered above are intended (as exact, of course) in his definition of a plane, which would otherwise be most obscure. Behold his definition (Book I, Definition 7, ed. Heiberg, pp. 2–3):

> ἐπίπεδος ἐπιφάνειά ἐστιν, ἥτις ἐξ ἴσου ταῖς ἐφ' ἑαυτῆς εὐθείαις κεῖται.
> 
> ("A plane surface is that which lies evenly with the straight lines on itself.")

### 6. On the Existence of the Straight Line and the Plane

We must now inquire whether exact figures—in the first place the straight line and the plane surface, whose notions we drew from inexact sensitive data—also truly exist mathematically; whether in extension as such, uniform, in "space," they are possible. The problem, therefore, is whether and by what means the human intellect in this point can by affirming transcend the defect of exactitude of sensibility.

Above we already saw that this inexactitude has already been overcome in these points:
1. In recognizing the existence of indivisibles under a certain respect or even simply, i.e., in recognizing the existence of surface, line, and point in the exact sense;
2. In the analysis of the continuity of extension as such, which teaches infinite divisibility, and the impossibility of composing extension out of inextensibles;
3. In recognizing extension, as it is extended, as homogeneous, uniform (or formless/amorphous),[^14] i.e., as the principle of individuation; from which immediately flows the possibility of congruent figures and the possibility of deducing total congruence if partial congruence is given; from which flows the possibility of motion of an invariable figure;
4. In the analysis of the notion of direction, where the analysis of the continuity of extension leads to recognizing infinitesimal direction and the existence of a continuum of directions around a point in a surface or in "space."

What we discovered regarding the exact notion of direction and regarding extension as the principle of individuation, if taken together, will also permit a legitimate transition from the inexactitude of sensibility to knowing the mathematical existence of exact figures.

#### A) On the Straight Line

Let us begin with the straight line. We saw how the idea of the direction of a line arises from the intuition of lines passing through a single point in diverse ways. We saw also how, to conceive direction exactly, one must pass to the limit through infinitesimal consideration. Lines that in this passage to the limit preserve diverse directions truly pass through that point according to diverse directions; others touch each other at this point and have the same direction there. In these notions we have already transcended the defect of exactitude of sensibility by employing the ideas of a truly unidimensional line, and of divisibility, which follows continuity, both in dividing lines themselves and in considering the continuous diversity of directions.

Furthermore, we saw that diversity of directions can be apprehended in lines as they pass through distant points; there we apprehend at least the notion (not yet the existence) of exactly similar directions. And applying these to diverse parts of a single line, we also apprehend the notion of a straight line, not yet its existence.

That both, however, also exist mathematically, extension as it is the principle of individuation again requires. In extension as such, which is not yet determined and bounded by figure, in pure "space," every part and every point is distinguished from others of its species by number alone; according to the structure of the surrounding extension, it is specifically identical with every other point. Hence every direction that exists at one point is repeated in each of the others as specifically the same; at individual points specifically the same continuum of directions exists. Hence it also immediately results that lines exist that in each of their parts have the same direction, according to all possible directions. Straight lines exist, therefore, and indeed in such a way that at each point a continuous sheaf of straight lines can be constructed.

We say that in extension—"space"—the matter stands thus; for again, as in the case of congruence, an extension must be supposed that is not yet determined by a figure. Thus on a spherical surface, at individual points (except those diametrically opposed), the same continuum of directions is not present; on the contrary, at each point a different one is present. On such a surface, on account of its curvature, straight lines cannot be given; indeed, their curvature consists in this. Nor can lines be given there whose curvature is less than that of great circles. The exactly infinitesimal directions that at one point of such a surface are situated in it, at other points (except one) are indeed present in the space in which that surface is a figure, but pass through the surface.

One intuitive truth regarding the connection of point and straight line must be touched upon here. The straight line, as it is a figure, can be moved in every way in uniform space. Let it be moved in such a way that one of its points remains fixed. It is manifest that the line can be so rotated as to attain any other point in space; whence we understand: through any point to any other point at least one straight line can be drawn; whether only one is not yet established.

#### B) On the Plane Surface

The first figure whose existence was discovered by us was the surface, as the boundary of the parts that arise by the first division of the extension originally given to us (which then revealed itself as extension of three dimensions). This figure was already immediately recognized as exact in this sense: that according to one dimension present in the original extension, it is indivisible, permitting a transition in an indivisible from one part to the other (Chapter III, § 2, no. 2 A). This figure must be examined with respect to other elements of exactitude, and specifically whether a plane surface, whose notion is already established, also exists mathematically; whether after the *quid est* of this proper attribute of surfaces (namely "planeness") it can also be established concerning the *an est*.

On a surface sensibly and clearly curved, these things are observed: it does not equally face the two parts of extension between which it is a boundary; toward one part it is concave, toward the other by that very fact it is convex. On the contrary, a sensibly plane surface faces both parts in the same way; from that datum arises the concept of an exactly plane surface. That it exists we immediately understand from the uniformity of extension as such, of "space"; again, because its parts are distinguished from one another by no distinction other than a numerical one, therefore a boundary between two parts can be given that faces them in perfectly the same way. And indeed according to a twofold respect:
1. So that this is verified in one part of the surface we are considering; and
2. So that the same can be verified in the same way in each of the other parts that are thus only numerically distinguished from one another.

The first is verified also in infinitesimal parts of a sphere; the second is not.

This diversity and identity of respects toward one and the other part of space can be more precisely determined, even in exact geometry. The individual points of a surface are, as was said above, apices of the continuum of directions found around those points in the surface. Insofar as a point is situated in the space divided by the surface, it is the apex of a three-dimensional continuum of directions that is split by the surface into two parts (besides those directions present in the surface itself). All these directions pass through the surface, but one class penetrates the surface, let us say from left to right, while the other contains the inverse directions that pass from right to left. (Lines that penetrate through the same point, each according to one direction in the line itself, belong to one class; according to the opposite direction, to the other). These things are also preserved in exact geometry, which can also consider directions whose passage cannot be sensibly observed. By means of these concepts we can more precisely determine that diversity and identity of respects.

Let there be a sensibly curved surface whose concave face faces the right part, and whose convex face therefore faces the left part of space; let there be a sensibly straight line (which can be exactly straight, for we already know it exists) that is vertical. This line, in the upward direction, first penetrates the surface from left to right; then, at a greater height, from right to left. The same direction in a single line, therefore, is related in an inverse manner to the surface at one point and at another point of the surface. This is what we were saying: the surface does not face one and the other part of space in the same way; and this remains true in exact geometry: relative to the same directions (of this straight line), the surface at diverse points is related in an opposite way.

A plane surface, therefore, which faces both parts of the space it divides in the same way, will be that which is related in the same way relative to exactly similar directions. And on account of the uniformity of extension as such, of space, such a surface exists also in exact geometry. In such a surface, the same directions pass through it from one part to the other, the same in the inverse manner, the same are situated in it; and that at each point.

### 7. On the Connections of Straight Line and Plane

What has been said in the last place regards the connections between the exact straight line and the exact plane, and immediately leads to various axioms. Only certain ones are to be expounded here.[^15]

1. The first that immediately follows is: a straight line that has two points in common with a plane cannot between those points be situated outside it. For the same direction of the straight line at one point would belong to directions that tend from one part of space to the other, while at another point it would belong to directions that tend in the inverse way; the surface would no longer be plane. The direction of the straight line is therefore situated in the plane itself, and consequently the entire straight line.

2. In the plane itself, however, through two points only a single straight line can be drawn; (that one is always present was already established before). For if two were supposed, at one of those points they would have diverse directions, which are constant in each straight line; constantly, therefore, would the directions differ. But at those two points the directions would be opposite in diverse ways; at one, the first would differ from the other, for example, by a deviation toward the right, at the other toward the left. At least one direction would not be constant. Unique, therefore, is the straight line that joins two points, and in the plane in which those points are situated, the entire straight line lies.

There follow certain things that employ the motion of figures, whose legitimacy was established earlier; we already employed it in considering a straight line moved so that one of its points remains fixed:

3. A plane containing a straight line can be rotated around it. In that motion it attains a unique position in which it contains a determinate point situated outside it; moreover, this point is arbitrary. Hence: **a straight line and a point situated outside it determine a plane**. Hence it is understood that a plane can be so described or arise: through the motion of a straight line drawn through any point, which is so moved as always to fall upon another straight line. The same plane is described through a similar motion of a straight line from any point along any straight line, provided they are situated in the first plane. Thus it is evident that all points of a plane are equivalent to one another, likewise all straight lines; not only relative to the parts of space that the plane bounds—that we already knew—but also relative to the plane itself. And perhaps we do not err in saying that this is the intention of Euclid in his definition cited above: "A plane surface is that which lies evenly with the straight lines on itself."
   
   A consequence of the greatest moment is: **The plane is homogeneous, uniform—that is, it is the principle of individuation for all figures situated in it.**

4. Thence it immediately follows that circles exist that can be described in a plane from any point as center and with any radius. The continuity of the circle is best understood from the continuity of motion of the point that describes it.

5. Thence now the notion of an **angle** can be determined. Already in inexact geometry we saw that from the diversity of direction of lines drawn in a single surface through the same point, there result "divergences" that, relative to each other, can be called greater and lesser. Now this notion of divergence in exact geometry can be polished into the notion of an angle: a quantitative notion that admits of greater and lesser and can be exactly measured. Straight lines passing through the same point in a plane, insofar as they differ more or less in direction, contain between them a greater or lesser part of the indefinite plane. These parts of the plane can be compared with each other; they can be measured exactly, for instance, by means of a circle whose center is that point and whose radius has the unit of length; sectors of this circle comprehended within the straight lines, or even the relative length of the arcs, are the exact measure of the divergence that will now be called "angle." Consequently, they are the measure of the diversity of directions, just as a quantitative effect is the measure of a qualitative cause.

Let us note especially what is essential in this entire analysis: **Because the plane is the principle of individuation, at any point an exactly similar diversity of directions causes exactly equal, congruent angles.**

Thence results a consequence of the greatest moment:
- In a plane there exist straight lines, not coinciding, that have an exactly similar direction; let us call these straight lines **parallels**.
- A parallel drawn in space through a point outside a given straight line is situated in the plane determined by that point and that straight line, for its direction cannot intersect the plane that contains that direction.
- Through any point outside a given straight line, there always exists one parallel; also **only one**; for a straight line diverse from this, passing through the same point, by that very fact has another direction. It is the principle of a unique parallel.
- But parallel straight lines, according to what we saw above concerning the plane as the principle of individuation, with any straight line intersecting them, because they have the same diversity of directions, make the same corresponding angles.

And this consequence constitutes Euclidean geometry in the pregnant sense, as opposed to modern, non-Euclidean geometries.

### 8. Again on the Notion of Direction

This conclusion of the greatest moment follows from the very notion of direction, as has already been observed by several thinkers. But they did not subject the notion to analysis, as we have done. Hence, and hence alone, it also happened that mathematicians raised a difficulty against the introduction of this notion. Before we proceed in our investigation, this must be examined.

One mode of proposing the difficulty we already encountered above in Helmholtz. It comes down to this: we define direction by means of the straight line; if, therefore, we define the straight line, as was done in our work, by means of direction, we are moving in a vicious circle.

These things would be true if de facto we defined direction thus. But we do not do so; we define it as the "mode of passage" of a line, even a curved one, through a point; and this is a truly primitive idea. For the notion of direction (still inexact), as is manifest from what has been said, arises immediately in the mind contemplating the simplest figure; likewise the notion of a straight line, in opposition to curves, as the notion of a line with constant direction, is conceived without any difficulty. There no difficulty lies hidden. It arises only if, after this question of the *quid est*, there arises the problem of the *an est*, both for the notion of direction and for the derived notion of the straight line. And to solve this difficulty there is required the intuition of extension as it is the principle of individuation, and the development and application of this notion. And, as seems to follow from the foregoing, the philosophical analysis of these notions and of this principle demonstrates that the human mind applies the same mode of procedure, but without analysis, using only virtual judgments. In such a mode of procedure no vicious circle is present, nor is it latently present there. And analysis demonstrates that the difficulty is not present in the notions, as the difficulty raised intimates, but in intuiting the mathematical existence of exact objects; we are dealing with the problem of exactitude, not in the notions, but in existence.

More copious and better are the reflections found in Wilhelm Killing, to whom we are also referred in Bertrand Russell.[^16] He says: 

> "The angle is the measure of the difference of direction of two straight lines; consequently the angles that two parallels make with the same straight line are equal."[^17]

He attributes this method to Leibniz; but he calls it worse than Euclid's mode of procedure, because Euclid indicates the difficulty that Leibniz clouds over.

To effect this, Killing begins by saying that the idea of direction is held by authors who employ it as primitive, because they do not give a definition. And it is very well considered thus, provided this notion is genetically developed and polished to exactitude. Then Killing urges, as is proper, the connection that the idea of direction has with the idea of angle. But he thinks that in this way a means of recognizing equal diversities of directions cannot be indicated without presupposing the whole theory of parallels. That this entire theory is involved in a suitable means of recognizing this equality is true; but he thinks equality of angles is not a suitable means, because it would presuppose this theory. And he intends to say that this theory is gratuitously presupposed; in which he is mistaken.

What the distinguished author intends is more clearly understood (along with his error) from what follows later. For he wishes, in order to expound Leibniz's idea further, to introduce this definition of direction: 

> "Two straight lines have similar or dissimilar direction if with a straight line intersecting both they make equal or unequal angles."[^18]

If this is truly used as a definition, what he adds is true: namely, that this does not lead to the goal—that is, to deducing the Euclidean theory of parallels—and therefore this theory would be gratuitously presupposed. For as he well continues:

> "It can only be said: those straight lines have similar or dissimilar direction relative to a determinate third straight line; but then it is uncertain whether two straight lines that make equal angles with a determinate straight line are also intersected by every straight line under equal angles."[^19]

These things are excellently and most truly said on the supposition of that proposition as a definition; if, however, it is a proposition that follows from the exact notion of direction established from elsewhere, they are no longer true. For from this follows the universal proposition: **every straight line makes equal corresponding angles with two straight lines of the same direction.**

And we expound this author and his difficulty so broadly especially because from it it is better understood that in our mode of procedure—which genetically discovers also the existence (as to the *an est*) of the exact notions: of direction, of the straight line as a line of constant direction, of the plane as the principle of individuation, of the angle as the measure of diversity of direction—we immediately arrive at the universal proposition: **every straight line makes equal corresponding angles with two straight lines of the same direction (parallels)**; just as there universally follows the inverse proposition: **if one straight line with two others situated in the same plane makes equal corresponding angles, these straight lines are parallel.**

And concerning this whole process it can be said: these things are indeed evident from experience in an approximate manner, but from the means employed of arriving at exact intellective cognition, it is evident that it is exact and necessary. The ultimate means of attaining exactitude is the intuition of extension as it is the principle of individuation. This principle implies not only specifically the same continuum of directions at individual points, but moreover the equality of angles resulting from specifically the same difference of directions. A special case of this principle is Euclid's Postulate IV on the equality of right angles. And all the means employed indeed require, in order to be explicitly detected, an accurate philosophical analysis; but these very means in constructing classical geometry were employed implicitly and naturally, as virtual judgments. Therefore this analysis manifests the mode of procedure of the human mind, *le cheminement de la pensée*.[^20]

### 9. On Direction in Euclid

It is rightly said, therefore, that the consequence we deduced above from the elaborated notion and mathematical existence of direction contains the Euclidean theory of parallels. Euclid himself, of course, does not use that notion in his theory; hence also his very definition of parallels is different (Book I, Definition 23): 

> "Parallel lines are straight lines that, being in the same plane and being produced indefinitely in both directions, do not meet one another in either direction."

He uses, therefore, a notion pertaining to connections (absence of concurrence) for the definition; hence also the famous Postulate V enunciates something concerning such a connection. But the same theory of parallels is deduced thence.

Hence it is no wonder that the notion of direction, and indeed of constant direction, as a proper attribute of the straight line, is found immediately after Postulate V is applied (in Proposition 30, immediately after 29, in which the first and only application of the postulate is found). For this proposition establishes: *if straight line $a$ is parallel to straight line $b$, and this to straight line $c$, then $a$ and $c$ also are parallel.* That the notion of (constant) direction as a proper attribute of the straight line is contained there is shown according to the **logic of relations**:

This relation (of parallelism), according to that proposition, is **transitive**. But concerning the same relation it holds: if $a$ is parallel to $b$, then $b$ will be parallel to $a$; it is a **symmetric** relation. Moreover: straight line $a$ can be said to be parallel to itself; it is a **reflexive** relation. Parallelism belongs, therefore, to the same genus of relations to which the relation of equality belongs. For if in quantities $a = b$ and $b = c$, it follows: $a = c$. Likewise: if $a = b$, it follows: $b = a$. Likewise it can be said: $a = a$. Both relations, therefore—of parallelism and of equality—are transitive, symmetric, and reflexive.

Just as the relation of equality supposes in its terms some proper attribute as a foundation, so the similar relation of parallelism supposes in its terms—in straight lines, namely—some proper attribute, and this is direction itself. Thus, therefore, in Euclid's mode of procedure there appears again the notion of constant and unique direction in a straight line, and the existence of a perfectly similar direction at distant points of a plane.

If "direction" is defined thus, then we cannot use that notion to define the straight line without a circle; and on this supposition, but on this alone, Helmholtz and others would speak rightly. But thence it is also manifest that this notion does not arise thus in the human mind; it apprehends it directly, as sensibly verified in lines; and this notion is elaborated to exactitude by other means than through the circumlocutions of Euclid's definition and postulate; it is already possessed from the fact that extension is known as the principle of individuation. And this is a principle that certainly is not usually enunciated, but is always present in the human mind while it considers the proper attributes of extension. Whence whoever examines the mode of knowing of the human mind cannot neglect it.

Regarding what was said above, two remarks may follow:

1. The first is this: from what we were saying according to the logic of relations, it does not follow that the relations of equality of quantities and parallelism of direction of lines are entirely identified with each other. Let another relation serve as an example, which in its own way is also transitive, symmetric, and reflexive: namely, the relation of perfect likeness between objects of qualities, e.g., of colors. There too it holds: if $a$ is like $b$, and $b$ like $c$, then $a$ is like $c$; simultaneously: if $a$ is like $b$, then $b$ is like $a$; and moreover it can be said: $a$ is like itself. Although, therefore, the relation of equality of quantities and of perfect likeness between qualities have the same three properties, their foundations, quantity and color, are nevertheless diverse and known as diverse. In the same way, the foundation of the relation of parallelism—namely, the direction of the straight line, which is characteristic for Euclidean geometry—is diverse, and known to us as diverse, from the foundations of those other relations. Which certain thinkers, applying the doctrine of the logic of relations, sometimes seem to forget.

2. The other remark is this: in the theory of a unique parallel, there returns the intuitive notion of direction, of constant direction, of the straight line that has this direction as a proper attribute. If, therefore, an extension exists that does not admit the Euclidean theory of a unique parallel, it does not admit constant direction, it does not admit truly straight lines; it will therefore be an extension that in the proper and intuitive sense is **curved**.
   
   Mathematical philosophers who determine curvature (which is defined according to Gauss) through intrinsic measures in extension (e.g., in a surface) and do not look to what can be outside it, sometimes say that this curvature is not curvature worthy of the name in the intuitive sense. If measures are considered in the customary and unchanged sense, they seem to err. For in that extension there is lacking "straightness," which implies constant direction; it is truly curved. And thus it necessarily supposes an extension of more dimensions, in which the directions lacking in the extension being measured are present; and this extension is curved in that higher extension in the proper sense.
   
   Moreover: surfaces are given that have "Gaussian" curvature of "value zero," which can therefore be applied to a plane, but which nevertheless have "intuitive" and true curvature (cylindrical and conical surfaces).

## § 5. ON EUCLIDEAN AND NON-EUCLIDEAN GEOMETRY

From our analysis there follows Euclidean geometry, and that as necessarily unique; which in our times can be regarded as a defect, indeed as an error. For besides Euclidean geometry, non-Euclidean geometry also—and indeed more than one—must be admitted as true. These indeed are sometimes called by mathematicians—at least so it happens in the Netherlands—"pathological geometries"; but this "pathology" does not regard the minds of the geometers who cultivate them, but rather the objects with which they deal. And these things are perhaps said rather jestingly; for mathematicians are accustomed to attribute the same value to non-Euclidean geometry as to Euclidean. Indeed, they say that anyone who calls Euclidean geometry uniquely necessary ought no longer to be read. The distinguished Felix Hausdorff says:

> "Mathematics may now archive unexamined any a priori construction that claims to deduce Euclidean space with its special properties as a thought-necessity, free of arbitrariness and without presuppositions."[^21]

These things were written in the year 1904; but even later similar statements are uttered. Today, however, the genuine character of non-Euclidean geometry is better known, and we too, after our deduction, attribute to non-Euclidean geometry a true and real meaning—indeed a meaning that extends more broadly than that which the cited author vindicates for it. Only we shall not be able to attribute the same realistic value to both geometries, Euclidean and non-Euclidean; and that not only in the practical order (as Poincaré did), but above all in the theoretical order and relative to the theory of cognition of the human mind. (See below, Chapter VI, § 1).

Non-Euclidean geometry indeed has a true and real meaning; indeed, it has several meanings. First among them is a certain logical meaning, and from it diverse real meanings can be derived; but there is none among them that could equate it with Euclidean geometry. Now we must show what errors we do *not* commit in asserting the necessity of the unique Euclidean geometry as the science of extension as it is extended, which was the conclusion from the foregoing.

* * *

We observe first: The error often attributed to those who assert the necessity of Euclidean geometry is this: they attempt to demonstrate Postulate V from the (sole) other axioms that Euclid admits, whether explicitly or implicitly. I do not know whether anyone truly exists who attempted to do this; if he exists, he certainly errs. For it has been demonstrated by mathematicians that those other axioms, united with a certain proposition denying Postulate V, cannot lead to a contradiction. This is properly that primary, purely logical meaning of non-Euclidean geometry. Hence it is certain that Postulate V is entirely independent of those other axioms, and therefore cannot be demonstrated from them alone. We in the foregoing did not attempt to do this; we indeed established the postulate, but by employing other means—primitive intuitions—besides the other axioms of Euclid.

From which it is also immediately evident that we do not commit another error often attributed to those who vindicate a unique position for Euclidean geometry: we do not say that Postulate V itself is *immediately evident*. Concerning this point, certain things must at least be sketched here.

What in the course of history was the difficulty that could be raised against Postulate V as a proposed first principle? It is this: the postulate affirms that two straight lines in one plane meeting a third line on that side of the plane divided by this third line meet where the sum of the interior angles they make with the third line is less than two right angles. This proposition, to anyone inspecting a figure in which this sum is far below this value, is of course immediately clear. But if it is only a very little below this value—and, on account of continuity, the difference can decrease into infinity down to zero—on account of the inexactitude of the senses it can no longer immediately be read intellectively in the figure; add that the point through which one of the two straight lines, almost parallel to the other, passes can be very distant from the other; and this distance can increase into infinity. It is manifest: it cannot in every case be established from mere inspection that those two straight lines fail to meet only in the case where that sum of angles attains two right angles. From mere inspection it is not established that this is not verified sooner. The exact proposition that affirms this—and this is Postulate V—cannot immediately through abstractive intuition be understood as true.

It is strange that so few mathematicians who treat Postulate V accurately expound this very difficulty. They are accustomed to employ expressions such as this: Postulate V is "less evident" than the other axioms of Euclid.[^22] They ought to say: *in its exactitude it is immediately entirely non-evident.*—Moreover, as follows from our exposition, the same must be said of the axiom of a unique straight line through two points; this too in its exactitude cannot be read immediately from the figure; in order to become evident, it needs another means of overcoming the inexactitude of sensibility.

On the contrary, the celebrated Felix Klein, as is his custom, expounds the difficulty with complete clarity;[^23] from him we have borrowed the formulation of the problem as described.

From his exposition it is manifest: we are dealing with our problem, with the problem of geometric exactitude arising from sensitive data. Hence it is also clear: it cannot be solved directly through intuitive abstraction; hence a means of indirect solution must be sought; which we attempted to do above.

If this means were not present or were not found, it would have to be said: it is not certain that extension (the plane) by its nature is Euclidean; it can happen that extensions are given that are not Euclidean, in which those two straight lines are no longer concurrent before the value of that sum of two angles attains two right angles.[^24] In the same extension, of course, Euclid's position and this other position are not verified simultaneously, for they are contradictory to each other. But a Euclidean plane could be given, and others, non-Euclidean in this sense. On this supposition it is also clear that diverse extensions of this second kind could be given that are related to one another in such a way that sooner or later the limiting position in which those two straight lines no longer meet is attained. Wherefore, if these extensions exist, they constitute an entire class containing infinitely many species, which differ from one another by their structure. This difference can be expressed by a certain factor, constant in individual species, continuously variable in the transition to another species; if this factor is sufficiently small, the extension determined by it does not sensibly differ, in a suitably chosen region, from Euclidean extension; this is the geometry that Lobachevsky discovered. The problem posed is again the problem of exactitude, and direct intuition of Postulate V cannot solve it.

We were saying that diverse extensions are possible; from what has been said so far, only in this sense: that the absence of internal contradiction, in the sense described, is established in those extensions of the other kind. But on this supposition the position of those mathematicians is understood who, like Gauss, wished to decide by experiment whether the real world was Euclidean or perhaps not.

These things must be said if another means of deciding the problem is not present—if Postulate V cannot be deduced as a necessary truth from others that are indeed immediately evident. In the course of history diverse means were proposed; and thence it is clear that at least those who proposed these did not admit the error mentioned earlier: they did not wish to demonstrate the postulate from the other axioms alone. But another error was present: the proposed means labored under the same defect as the postulate itself; nor were these means, on account of the inexactitude of the senses, sufficient. We indicate two:
1. The first means is the proposition that affirms: *non-equal triangles exist whose sides are proportional*. This is truly equivalent to Postulate V. But neither is this proposition immediately evident; for the senses, of course, such triangles can be given; but thence, on account of the inexactitude of sensibility, it does not follow that this proportionality is verified exactly.
2. The other is that attributed to Clavius: as a definition it is assumed that *the locus of points equidistant from a straight line is parallel*. This locus is indeed sensibly a straight line; but that it is exactly straight cannot be established from intuition alone; again on account of the defect of exactitude in sensitive data, which cannot be directly overcome.

In place of these notions we used the notion of direction; but this too has already been introduced rather often to decide our problem—among philosophers, for instance, by Charles Renouvier. And there indeed this is immediately evident: through a point situated outside a straight line, only a single line of exactly the same direction can be drawn. But if this notion is not genetically explained, and its existence, together with the existence of the straight line and the plane and their connections, is not understood and examined by means of the principle of individuation, it is subject to the difficulties we heard set forth earlier by mathematicians. Hence we examined this notion and these existences with all care. And in our mode of developing the notion of exact direction, those difficulties, as we saw, do not subsist; and the whole process seems to solve the problem. Whence we have not committed this third error either: we did not use, in place of Postulate V, another axiom equivalent to it, but laboring under the same defect of absence of immediate evidence.

What, therefore, is to be said of those extensions described by Klein that can be sensibly Euclidean, yet in reality, in their internal and exact structure, are not Euclidean? We certainly do not deny that they can exist. Indeed, their existence was already discovered by Eugenio Beltrami in his "pseudosphere," and this figure was elaborated by Helmholtz as an example of a non-Euclidean geometry in which Postulate V does not hold; if its curvature is sufficiently small, in a suitably chosen finite part it cannot sensibly be distinguished from a Euclidean plane. Yet this geometry is not to be equated with Euclidean geometry. It is a special case of this very geometry; such a "plane" is a curved Euclidean surface; and, as was already demonstrated above, this curvature is to be understood in the intuitive sense; it supposes other dimensions according to which the figure is curved. This has already been said by several thinkers; but to demonstrate it fully, we need the notion of exact direction, fully elaborated. Hence this notion, which we showed to be primitive, is of such great moment.[^25]

We add one thing briefly: in the manner described, non-Euclidean geometry of two dimensions is real; whether in similar fashion three-dimensional non-Euclidean geometry is real depends evidently upon the solution of the problem whether a "space" having more than three dimensions is possible. That such a geometry contains no internal contradiction, so that from the posited axioms contradictory propositions cannot follow if we proceed purely logically, is already established.

* * *

In this sense, therefore, Euclidean geometry is necessary and unique. Alongside it, to be sure, a geometry can be constructed that uses the same terms and the same axioms as classical geometry, with the sole exception of Postulate V, in whose place another, contradicting it, is posited. This geometry logically leads to no contradiction; indeed, extensions can be given, sensibly not differing from Euclidean ones, in which they are verified. But by that very fact the exact meaning of the terms and axioms that are retained has been changed. They can indeed have a real meaning, even a geometric meaning; thus in the case considered above their meaning is very close to the classical meaning of the notions, and can sensibly coincide with it. But this case deals with nothing other than a Euclidean extension curved according to a further dimension. Which cannot be a matter of wonder, because it deals with an extension that precisely relative to constant directions is no longer the principle of individuation.

Thus Euclidean geometry can be unique, and yet admit non-Euclidean geometry, indeed non-Euclidean extensions.

## § 6. ON NON-EUCLIDEAN GEOMETRY RELATIVE TO THE COGNITION OF THE HUMAN MIND

From the foregoing it is easy to solve the difficulty raised against the theory of knowledge, indeed against the very value of the cognition of the human mind, which through so many centuries contended that extension was necessarily Euclidean. This difficulty is usually opposed to the Kantian theory of cognition; certain of the neo-Kantians (such as Ernst Cassirer) acknowledge it, while others attempt to refute it. But if the difficulty is rightly sustained, it holds not so much against the Kantian theory as against all theories that recognize classical geometry as a necessary science; and those who raise it intend rather to affirm this. Indeed, as was said, it would hold against the very nature of the human mind, insofar as it admits necessary cognition; for the mind itself through so many centuries would have led man into error.

Against Kant's theory we can rather argue from geometry in another way. For Kant in his theory thought, without justification, that Euclidean geometry was justified; hence, if he admitted it as unique, he did so rather on account of reasons borrowed not from his theory, but from the natural conviction of the human race. This was already noted above (Chapter III, § 2, no. 5). Kantian extensions, as was said there, lack exactitude; and Kant himself completely ignored the problem of exactitude (Chapter I, § 5); but from this problem arises the question of non-Euclidean geometry. From this ignorance it also seems to come about that Kant recognized only sensitive intuition, not intellective intuition; this intellective intuition, however, is evidently proved from the problem of exactitude having been overcome. Let it suffice to have intimated these things briefly.

If, however, the difficulty of the moderns can be urged against the very nature of the human mind, it must certainly be solved by every other theory of cognition that admits real objects, not merely phenomenal ones, of geometry—whether these objects are found in bodies themselves, as Aristotle holds, or exist separated, as Plato wills. For these theories admitted Euclidean geometry as necessary and unique.

From what was said above, the objection is easily solved. First we ask: What did the geometers constructing classical geometry intend? Using the terms "straight line" and "plane," they intended to speak of those exact figures whose notion is easily discovered, whose mathematical existence, according to all that it implies, becomes manifest from the analysis given above. And if someone, by the terms he uses, intends to deal with these exact objects, he rightly asserts that these objects necessarily and uniquely obey classical geometry; there there is no error; concerning these objects no other geometry holds; modern critique has demonstrated this. That analysis is indeed long if all elements are to be expounded separately and clearly; but the analysis itself also shows that those elements of exactitude are always present, in virtual judgments, to the human mind operating naturally; the sole difficulty is found in elaborating them; (concerning this matter, more in the following section). The human race did not err, therefore, in asserting Euclidean geometry as unique, nor in the (practical) exclusion of other geometries implicitly contained there. For it intended to deal with the straight line and the plane in the sense described; and those things described by these terms in the new geometries are other objects.

Secondly, however, according to what was expounded above, even more must be said: these other objects are not excluded by classical geometry. For those objects, even those sensibly similar to the objects of classical geometry (and these are dealt with in the problem of exactitude), are nothing but a part of the objects of classical geometry; they are in reality curved objects in a higher space. Therefore it must more truly be said: in classical geometry they were not excluded, but not yet examined; therefore they pertain rather to those things newly discovered in the course of the evolution of geometry; prior ignorance of them certainly does not constitute an error.

Among the objects newly discovered, however, non-Euclidean extensions have something very peculiar; and thence the origin of our difficulty is understood. It is this: the properties of non-Euclidean extensions can be deduced from axioms expressed in entirely the same terms as the Euclidean axioms of the straight line and the plane, with the sole exception of Postulate V, in whose place Lobachevsky was able to posit another, contradicting it. This is a very marvelous property, and its discovery is a work of the greatest merit. Thence it is also understood why geometers attempted to equate the new geometry with Euclidean geometry; thence arises the opinion that raises a difficulty against classical theories of cognition that hold Euclidean geometry to be necessary and unique. Thus it is understood why this difficulty could arise, but simultaneously it is understood that it has no validity.

We say that the other axioms—namely, those preceding Postulate V—are expressed in both geometries in entirely the same terms. Thence, as has already been said, it does not follow that they have the same meaning. We have already seen that classical geometry attributes to the terms "straight line" and "plane" the exact meaning we explained earlier; these words, however, cannot retain the same meaning in the new geometries. For from these exact concepts Postulate V necessarily flows, as has been shown.

Not on that account, however, can it be said that this postulate logically, by means of a syllogism, follows from the other axioms. It must only be said: these other axioms do not yet express all the proper attributes of "straight line" and "plane" that we intuitively know concerning these objects; that these proper attributes may be expressed, we need Postulate V or something equivalent to it. In this sense, and in this sense alone, can we approve what Poincaré says: namely, that Postulate V contains (or rather perfects) the definition of the "straight line"; we add: and of the "plane." In this sense, namely, do we admit it: those things that we intuitively understand (by means of the notion of direction, for this is necessary) regarding the straight line, we do not yet fully express by the other axioms; that a full expression of them may be had, the addition of Postulate V is required.

Let the conclusion of this section, therefore, be: the entire enigma of the possibility of new geometries that seem capable of being constituted alongside classical geometry, so as to be equated with Euclidean geometry, and from which at first sight it seems to follow that the human race erred in admitting classical geometry as unique and necessary—this entire enigma consists in this: those geometries can be deduced from a system of propositions expressed in the same words as the Euclidean axioms, one or another being excepted that contradict certain axioms. This truth is certainly most worthy of attention, and its recognition constitutes an admirable progress, but it contains no mental calamity.[^26]

## § 7. ON CLASSICAL GEOMETRY RELATIVE TO THE PHILOSOPHY OF COGNITION

Classical geometry having been vindicated from the errors unjustly attributed to it, we can safely use it to understand from its structure the mode of procedure of the human mind. This consideration pertains to the philosophy of cognition, which ought above all to be examined in that most suitable object—namely, in that which is the very "proper object" of the human mind. Those things discovered in our analysis constitute a field of broad investigation; we shall now attempt to indicate at least certain general points.

We found a certain axiom in classical geometry that is not immediately evident, which is nevertheless posited without demonstration in the beginnings of geometry: Postulate V or its equivalent. And from our analysis it has been established that this axiom must be sustained; other geometries, insofar as they are truly geometries—sciences of extended being as such—are not to be equated with Euclidean geometry, but are truly parts of it; they follow, therefore, from Postulate V if the terms are taken in their natural sense. We posit, therefore, in the beginnings of geometry, a certain truth that is not immediately evident, which can and must be justified by mental operations instituted before the very beginnings of geometry.

Properly speaking, we found more than one truth of this kind. To the same kind belong:
- The axiom establishing that two points always determine one straight line (and this straight line, if "bounded," can be "produced continuously in a straight line");
- The axiom establishing that in a plane "from any center and radius a circle can be described"; and these three are the first "postulates" of Euclid.
- His fourth postulate also, which affirms that "all right angles are equal to one another," is of the same nature; something similar to this postulate we found in showing that equal corresponding angles arise in every part of any plane if a straight line meets other straight lines parallel to one another.
- The mathematical existence as well—not the notions themselves—of the straight line and the plane pertains to these truths.

All these regard the problem of exactitude, and indeed especially its second part. Of the axioms employed in geometry, only certain ones pertaining to "order" and to "connection"—such as those regarding the notion "between" and the position of a point on a line—are immediately evident; and these up to the preceding century were not even accustomed to be explicitly enunciated.

It is manifest, therefore, that in classical geometry certain truths are employed as "first principles" that are not immediately evident, which need deduction and can be deduced. Observing this, we recall what we were taught earlier (§ 1) by Aristotle. Let us resume these things according to the explanation of St. Thomas.

In *Posterior Analytics* I, 2 and 10, Aristotle distinguishes "axioms" (*dignitates*, ἀξιώματα) from "hypotheses" (*suppositiones*, ὑποθέσεις) and "postulates" (*petitiones*, αἰτήματα):
- To the first class belong those principles that have this proper attribute: "that it is not only necessary that they be true per se, but it is also necessary that they seem to be true per se. For no one can hold opinions contrary to them" (St. Thomas, lect. 19, no. 2; Aristotle, 76b23: ὃ ἀνάγκη εἶναι δι’ αὑτὸ καὶ δοκεῖν ἀνάγκη).
- Suppositions and postulates have this in common: "that although they are demonstrable, yet the demonstrator takes them without demonstrating them, and especially because they are not demonstrable through his own science, but through another" (ibid., lect. 19, no. 4; Aristotle, 76b27: δεικτὰ ὄντα λαμβάνει αὐτὸς μὴ δείξας).
- Between themselves, however, they differ: "for if indeed such a proposition is acceptable (*probabilis*) to the learner to whom the demonstration is made, it is called a supposition (*suppositio*)... If, however, he holds neither that opinion nor the contrary, the demonstrator must request this of him, and then it is called a postulate (*petitio*). If, however, he holds the contrary opinion, then it will be a question (*quaestio*)" (St. Thomas, ibid.; Aristotle, ibid., 28: ταῦτ’, ἐὰν μὲν δοκοῦντα λαμβάνῃ τῷ μανθάνοντι, ὑποτίθεται... ἂν δὲ ἢ μηδεμιᾶς ἐνούσης δόξης ἢ καὶ ἐναντίας ἐνούσης λαμβάνῃ τὸ αὐτὸ, αἰτεῖται).

This doctrine especially regards the master teaching the disciple; the difficulties[^27] and the difference indicated here we neglect; moreover it is manifest that this division, in diverse cases of "disciples," leads to diverse outcomes. But in general the things we discovered above are described thus.

* * *

To the class of "dignities" (*dignitates*, self-evident axioms) will pertain those things that regard the divisibility of extended entities, then the existence of indivisibles, and certain axioms of order and connection, which on that account were not even enunciated in antiquity. Euclid too does not enunciate them, and on that account is sharply carped at by certain thinkers—and indeed for the most part undeservedly, indeed erroneously. Among them again Bertrand Russell stands out, from whose critique we already saw certain points above. Regarding the very first proposition of the *Elements*, he writes thus:[^28]

> "There is no evidence whatever that the circles which we are told to construct intersect, and if they do not, the whole proposition fails. Euclid's problems are often regarded as existence-theorems, and from this point of view, it is plain, the assumption that the circles in question intersect is precisely the same as the assumption that there is an equilateral triangle on a given base."

Euclid is carped at because without argument he assumes that those two circles intersect; which he manifestly does, for he speaks simply of "the point $C$ in which the circles intersect one another." Then Russell (with many others) asserts that Euclid in this proposition only wishes to demonstrate the mathematical existence of an equilateral triangle (on a straight line of any length in any site as a side).[^note] That this is entirely true seems so to us as well; St. Thomas, moreover, already understood Euclid's intention in the same way (see *In Post. Anal.* I, lect. 2, no. 5).

And therefore Euclid is said by Russell to have been able with equal right (i.e., without justifying foundation) to suppose the existence of this triangle directly, without this argumentation or construction. This assertion, however, is erroneous; it ignores the problem of exactitude or the suitable mode of solving it. For from the sole intuition of a sensibly equilateral triangle, we cannot by right affirm that an exactly equilateral triangle mathematically exists; this direct transition to the affirmation of exactitude is manifestly illegitimate. But that those circles, constructed in that manner (whose existence is established from Postulate III), intersect each other is a truth that we can immediately, from sensible intuition, affirm with an intellective intuition. Hence Euclid, in place of a prior exact abstraction that could not be justified, with the best right posits another that is immediately manifest to the understanding mind. This second abstraction introduces an axiom pertaining to "connections."

Hence it is manifest that Russell wrongly identifies both cases. And this example illustrates the case of Postulate V and similar ones; in these postulates too, which are truly "petitions" and not self-evident, we can by other means overcome the inexactitude of sensibility, but not through direct abstraction. And this is the first fruit of the epistemological examination of Euclid's argumentation.

Another is also present: Does Euclid sin by not explicitly and beforehand enunciating this axiom (of the existence of the point of intersection of those circles) that he employs? This is often said by extreme logicians; undeservedly, however, do they demand this everywhere. First it must be said: if this is demanded, Euclid ought to have premised a general proposition (namely: two circles intersect each other if the distance of their centers is less than the sum of their radii and greater than their difference); but this proposition is not an axiom. And the truth that Euclid uses to establish the existence of the point of intersection becomes immediately evident, for this special case, at the moment the circles are described; nor does the evidence become greater if it is explicitly stated. Whence his mode of procedure seems to be more conformable to the nature of the mind, in which that intuition is present, determining the further progress in reasoning as a virtual judgment. We intuitively understand that those circles intersect each other; without explicitly affirming this, we use, in developing the argument, the existence of the point of intersection.

Then another virtual judgment is also present: Because the point of intersection $C$ is given, it must have the proper attributes of a point: namely, it can be joined by a straight line with any other point (this was Postulate I); again: without explicitly affirming this (or referring us to Postulate I), Euclid immediately proceeds by saying: "to points $A, B$ let straight lines $CA, CB$ be drawn." And so there is again present a virtual judgment, which is not expressed, but which nevertheless determines progress in the argument. Euclid's mode of arguing is an excellent example of the mode of procedure of the human mind.

Such as is the intuitive affirmation of the intersection of those two circles, such are the principles that can neither be demonstrated nor need demonstration. But there is especially the question concerning other geometric principles that indeed can be proved, but in geometry itself are not proved but supposed. How do these noetically differ from geometric conclusions—that is, from those propositions of geometry itself that are indeed demonstrated in it?

To detect this difference, certain points must be sampled regarding the distinction that Aristotle and the Scholastics customarily establish between **science strictly so called** (*scientia stricte dicta*), which is the habit of conclusions, and **understanding strictly so called** (*intellectus stricte dictus*), which is the habit of immediately evident principles:
- Science strictly so called, by means of the syllogism, deduces conclusions from premises; it uses a middle term between the extreme terms of the conclusion.
- Understanding strictly so called understands without a middle between the extremes of the principle.

In order that a syllogism be truly a demonstration, it is required that the premises be necessary and that the form of the syllogism be correct—that is, there is required the necessity of connection, by means of the middle term, between the premises; there is required both the necessity of matter, which is the necessity of principles, and the necessity of the form of the syllogism itself, the necessity of consequence (*necessitas consequentiae*).

To anyone paying careful attention to both necessities, however, there manifests itself a certain wonderful diversity, which is not usually examined and yet is of the greatest moment:

We can express individual propositions, in order to urge their form alone, by means of symbols, e.g., letters; then we use "transcendent terms" (*termini transcendentes*), as St. Albert the Great says;[^29] so Aristotle already did, and since him this use has become universal. Thus we can express the syllogism: *Every $M$ is $P$; every $S$ is $M$; therefore every $S$ is $P$* (or *All $S$ is $M$, all $M$ is $P$, therefore all $S$ is $P$*). This formula expresses the necessity of connection between the conclusion and the premises taken together, and therefore between $S$ and $P$, by means of the middle $M$. The necessity of form, or of consequence of the syllogism, is thus expressed.

Now to anyone paying attention this is manifest: **the necessity of this formal connection, or consequence, remains after the transition to the use of symbols, which has already been effected in the formula.** We can also express this intuition by a symbolic formula: *If $S$ is $M$ and $M$ is $P$, then $S$ is $P$.* This expresses the necessary connection between consequent and antecedent—a necessity, I say, whose intellective intuition is preserved even if we use these symbols and ignore their specific meaning.[^30] (Cf. below, Chapter V, § 2).

Hence it follows: in constructing a syllogism whose form is correct, it is no longer necessary to attend to the meaning of the terms in order to affirm the truth and necessity of the conclusion. It suffices that:
1. The terms have a meaning (even one unknown to us);
2. Next, that the connection between the extremes of the premises be necessary (without this necessity being currently seen);
3. Next, that terms occurring multiple times have the same meaning in each case, and that the functions of subject and predicate be distinguished.

In a simple syllogism, therefore, attention must be paid to ensuring that $M$ has the same meaning in both premises, and that to terms $S$ and $P$ in the conclusion the same meaning is attributed that they had in the premises.

The matter does not stand thus in the understanding of principles (*in intellectu principiorum*). Evident is this truth: *every extended entity is divisible*; but if we pass to the use of transcendent terms: *every $A$ is $B$*, the intuition of necessity immediately disappears, even though we intend to express the same principle. This disparity between the syllogism and the simple principle is of the greatest moment:
- We cannot establish a principle if we do not intuit necessity flowing from the meaning; hence the Scholastics were accustomed to say: principles are known "the terms being understood" (*intellectis terminis*).
- But we can know a new conclusion as necessary, provided the form of the syllogism—which we always intuit even in transcendent terms—is correct, provided we hold in memory that the premises—in the meaning the terms have—are necessary; hence this ultimate necessity does not need to be intuited in the act of concluding.

Hence it comes about that the work of science strictly so called—the science of conclusions—already differs greatly from the work of understanding strictly so called:
- For the former, the intuition of the correctness of the syllogistic form with the memory of the necessity of the premises suffices;
- For understanding, the intuition of this very necessity is always required.

Hence: in constructing science strictly so called, we are accustomed above all to use the intuition of the correctness of the syllogistic form, of the consequence. Indeed, thence is understood the attempt of so-called axiomatics to reduce the operations of science to these intuitions alone, to have which it suffices to suppose axioms without caring about their meaning. The former is already found in the doctrine of Aristotle, who distinguishes science from the understanding of principles, in whom therefore the beginnings of a certain axiomatics are present; the latter is fully had in modern axiomatics, which, as we were taught by Hilbert (Chapter I, § 6; cf. Chapter IV, § 4, no. 1), abstracts from the meaning of terms.[^31]

Aristotle differs, however, from the moderns; for although in science we especially use the intuition of syllogistic form, yet we do not neglect the meaning of terms, and thus a certain intellective intuition—greater, lesser, or even total—is always present; and understanding of the meaning of terms and propositions is never lacking; these things we already intimated earlier (Chapter I, § 5), and we shall see them again in Chapter V.

That conclusions are necessary, therefore, we always understand, even if we have used premises whose necessity we no longer intuit, but understood previously and hold in memory. Some intuition of necessity is always required, but in the work of constructing science the intuition of consequence suffices, which we understand in the syllogistic form expressed in words, even if the meaning of the words is hidden from us. Hence is evident the great utility of the syllogism.

What we were saying regards the necessity of propositions. But in our case—the case of geometry—from what was expounded above concerning the problem of exactitude, there follows an even much greater difference between science and the understanding of principles:

In this case, namely, to understand a principle intuitively, it is required that we transcend the inexactitude of the senses; sometimes this is easier, sometimes difficult, as is abundantly clear from what was expounded above; this difficulty is proper to geometry. But behold: **this difficulty in constructing science strictly so called now disappears.**

Attending to syllogistic form in a syllogism expressed in words, we certainly need the intuition of this form as it lies concretely in terms and propositions, but through this intuition the problem of exactitude no longer has to be overcome. For the form of the syllogism expressed in words, like the first arithmetic data (cf. Chapter I, § 3; Chapter II, § 1; Chapter III, § 1, no. 1), is already exact; hence there only the necessity of connection—that is, of consequence—must be recognized by the intellect; the problem of exactitude has entirely disappeared.

For if the exactitude of notions and connections is already present and affirmed in the premises—originally, therefore, in the axioms—by that very fact it is transferred into the conclusions. Hence, in the operations of science strictly so called, the difficulty of overcoming inexactitude is no longer found. Hence the greatest problem that arises in the noetic explanation of geometric cognition—the problem, I say, of exactitude—no longer presents itself in the syllogistic deduction of theorems from premises; this problem in its entirety is situated in the noetic explanation of the origin of the axioms themselves, from which syllogistic deduction takes its beginning.

In geometry, therefore, there is especially the greatest discrimination between the understanding of principles and science strictly so called, the science of conclusions.

Thence it is also better understood why modern axiomatics arose among mathematicians: axioms are purely supposed—more or less the same as are present in classical science—and mathematical science strictly so called is restricted to syllogistic deduction alone. But if the realistic value and truth of the axioms are not beforehand placed in safety thus, by that very fact there arise, as is clear, at least two problems:
1. Whether the supposed system of axioms is immune from internal contradiction—that is, whether contradiction is excluded in correct syllogistic deduction;
2. If this is established, there arises the problem whether the "science" so constructed can truly be applied to real extension; not only to physical extensions, but also to pure extension—to the entities from abstraction (τὰ ἐξ ἀφαιρέσεως) according to Aristotle; in other words: whether this science is truly geometry.

Classical theory, as understood by Aristotle in his theory of cognition, is at first sight, as has already been said, quite similar to axiomatics. For it separates the understanding of principles from the science of conclusions. But there is a great difference. For it remits the examination of principles to another science—philosophy—and thus wishes beforehand to justify them. But moreover in science itself, in deduction itself, it differs from modern axiomatics:

As we saw, it wishes so to deduce science that in scientific deduction it always holds in mind and understands the meaning of terms and relations. This was already said concerning the element of necessity; this must now be repeated concerning the element of exactitude. Nor on that account in this mode of procedure will the problem of exactitude have to be solved anew in deduction itself, although it understands that meaning in the phantasm. For, as is abundantly clear from what was said above: exact notions are very easily conceived; likewise the concept of exact relations is very easy; difficulties were present only—now lesser, now greater—in detecting the mathematical existence of these notions and relations in extension.

But behold: in deducing conclusions by means of the syllogism from exact axioms already established, and indeed in such a deduction that we always keep present the meaning of the words, we need only exact notions concerning whose existence it is already established. For concerning the existence of exact primary objects and connections it is already established from the study of principles:
- Concerning the existence of indivisibles: surface, line, point;
- Concerning the existence of congruent figures;
- Concerning the existence of the straight line, the plane, parallelism.

And this existence was already affirmed in the axioms, and this legitimate affirmation legitimately also passes into the conclusions without the problem of exactitude arising again; for to establish legitimate conclusions we need only the intuition into syllogistic form, which, as expounded, is free from the problem of exactitude. To these things we shall return in Chapter V.

Whence even in the Aristotelian conception of geometry there remains a huge discrimination between the noetic difficulties of the cognition of principles and of conclusions, and their examination is legitimately assigned to diverse sciences.

* * *

The science that justifies principles in Plato was dialectic, in Aristotle was metaphysics, in St. Thomas was either metaphysics or natural philosophy; let us say in general: it is philosophy (see above, Chapter I, § 1). And such a science today is again admitted under the name of "pre-geometric science" or "prior science" (*science préalable*) or "dialectic." Let us call it **noetics**.

A certain preliminary difficulty could arise here from what we have seen: in this prior, philosophical science also, formal syllogisms occur; and concerning these it will be possible to say what was said concerning geometric syllogisms: they suppose only the intuition of syllogistic form, even expressed in words, and participate in the facility of this intuition; therefore they do not differ from geometric syllogisms.

Let us say first: there sometimes without doubt such syllogisms occur, which scarcely differ at all from purely scientific syllogisms. Thence also it is that the system of axioms posited as the beginning of geometry is not prescribed in every respect by nature; in one way or another a suitable system can be chosen. Thus we, from the analysis of the notion of direction, arrived at another definition and other axioms of parallelism than those that occur in Euclid. Our system is better adapted to solving modern difficulties; moreover, it seems to be more natural; Euclid's axiom, however—from which immediately, as was said above, the whole theory of parallels and the very notion of the straight line's direction results—is very suitably posited on account of its simple content (it deals, as was said, with a certain connection), but it offered occasion for those difficulties.

Secondly, however, it must be said: even if here and there some formal syllogism occurs, indeed perhaps is necessary, the entire process is nevertheless not formally syllogistic; often it is non-syllogistic. Often its "movement" is the inverse of the syllogistic movement (cf. Chapter II, § 3, no. 3). It consists rather in an analysis—not indeed of terms, for this leads only to analytic judgments in the Kantian sense, which do not have great value—but in the **analysis of the very processes of the human mind**. This method is truly analysis; for it inquires into those operations by which the human mind in its natural mode of procedure—antecedently to philosophy—constructs geometry, and indeed in such a way that this is critically justified as a science. Difficulties arise only if, in the philosophical examination of the origin of this science, those processes that are truly present there and justify science are not found—if the analysis is poorly instituted. Briefly we indicate here certain things concerning the nature of these processes:

Among them are certain ones that are not formal syllogisms, but virtual ones only; just as virtual judgments occur, so also **virtual syllogisms** undoubtedly occur in the mode of procedure of the human mind:

A virtual judgment is present where the mind, having intuited a necessary connection between $S$ and $M$, does not explicitly affirm it: "$S$ is $M$," but proceeding to further things, intuits the necessity of the connection between $M$ and $P$; from both intuitions, gathered into one, results the cognition of the necessity of the (indirect) connection between $S$ and $P$; and this can immediately be affirmed. In this movement of the mind two virtual judgments are present; formally, only the third connection is affirmed. This process constitutes a virtual syllogism, whose existence, simultaneously with the existence of virtual judgments, is thus detected. The affirmation that follows after such a syllogism is an actual judgment. We can, of course, then reflecting, explicitly construct the syllogism, for all its materials are present; but what is contained in the natural movement of the mind is an operation very close to the operation that brings forth a first principle; it agrees with the syllogism only in that it contains a progressive movement of the mind.

That this movement can be rather the inverse of the syllogistic movement we already saw earlier (Chapter II, § 3, no. 3). For first we detect a specific truth (that two numbers can be added, and from their addition a number results); the reason why these are affirmed is detected to flow from a certain formal connection that can be enunciated in a causal proposition, and from this a more universal, generic truth is understood. The syllogistic movement leads from generic truth to specific truth. That the same is verified in many processes that we found in examining geometric principles, the reader will easily be able to see.

Regarding these processes of the human mind, it can further be observed: rather often, to discover principles, we must consult sensitive data—"inspect in the phantasm," as St. Thomas says; no longer in the phantasm of words, as can be done in the syllogism, but in the phantasm of things. But with this sensitive intuition there is always, as we have seen, associated an intellective intuition, which justifies both necessity and exactitude. Thus we detect:
- The divisibility of extension as it is extended, from the inspected nature of extension;
- The existence of indivisibles—surface, line, point—from understood continuity;
- Its infinite divisibility, from the same nature of continuity;
- The existence of congruent figures, then of exact figures, from the understood nature of extension as it is the principle of individuation.

These intellective intuitions, which are always joined together with sensitive intuition, are accurately detected and explicitly affirmed in analysis; but in the natural process of the human mind, as is clear from the same analysis, they are present without being explicitly affirmed, and indeed as motives determining assent to the principles. The principles, therefore, that are thus affirmed as necessary and exact suppose both the inspection of the phantasm—and thus are entirely like absolutely first principles—and abstraction, from which is had the intellective intuition of the very nature of extension. These processes, however, as is manifest, are not syllogistic; they are direct, intuitive-abstractive processes; they are of another genus than the syllogistic processes that constitute science strictly so called, the science of conclusions. It is long to examine this in particulars; there a broad field of noetic investigation lies open. More in Chapter V.

If, therefore, this natural mode of procedure of the mind is itself subjected to analysis, this no longer pertains to geometric science, but rather to the theory of cognition, to philosophy. This noetic science, moreover, can be reduced to metaphysics; for in all these cases we are dealing first with knowing the essential relations of the nature of extension in general, of the nature of diverse extensions in species; next with recognizing these relations and their foundations as beings, on account of their intelligibility. And so this noetic analysis can be reduced to metaphysics; or, because extension is treated, to natural philosophy. It is certainly a philosophical discipline. And the analysis is an **"operative analysis"** (*analysis operativa*).

In the foregoing we touch upon several points that seem to demand ampler investigation; concerning these we shall treat in the following chapter, which will deal with certain axiomatic questions. One remark must already be added here. We have spoken of symbolic expressions of propositions and of the syllogism that preserve the form of consequence, so that we can read its necessity in the formulation itself. This possibility would not imply much for making deduction if, as we did above, we employ only the purely "predicative" proposition "$S$ is $P$." If, however, we also use symbolic expressions of relations (between two and especially between more subjects), ample deduction becomes possible.

Above (Chapter IV, § 4, no. 9) we found that diverse real relations have similar logical properties: relations of quantitative equality, of qualitative likeness, of likeness of direction have this in common: that they are transitive, symmetric, reflexive. These relations seem to agree in logical properties, yet are really diverse and known as diverse. In Euclid's theory, the notion of direction is detected only through this logical process; in our method, in the inverse manner, we arrive from the notion of direction at the justification of the postulate. The study of these relations will be able to detect elements suitable for illustrating the doctrine of analogy. Other relations differ in their very logical properties. All these things, therefore, are worthy of deeper investigation.
