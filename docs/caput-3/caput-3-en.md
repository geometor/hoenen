## On the Problem of Exactitude

### PART I: ON THE EXISTENCE OF INDIVISIBLES

We must now address the second fundamental problem that requires resolution: the **problem of exactitude**, through which geometry differs fundamentally from elementary arithmetic (wherein exactitude constitutes no enigma). 

Not all aspects involved in this problem demand the same means of solution, as will become evident from our exposition. Certain questions require the consideration of extension as the "principle of individuation," while others are independent of that consideration. It is with these latter questions that the present chapter deals; the former questions—those depending upon the principle of individuation—will be expounded in the subsequent chapter.

---

### § 1. On the Formulation of the Problem

#### 1. In What the Problem Consists
Let us recall from earlier chapters in what the problem of exactitude consists: 

Whatever geometry affirms concerning its objects—points, lines, surfaces, their mutual relations, and the forms of figures (such as the straightness of a line, the equality or proportionality of figures)—is regarded as **absolutely exact**. 

Yet the sensitive data from which classical geometry historically derives its origin **entirely lack this exactitude**:
* Neither the external senses nor the imagination can perceive indivisible points or widthless lines, but only bodies extended in three (or at least two) dimensions.
* They cannot distinguish imperceptibly curved lines from straight ones; consequently, they can never determine empirically whether a given physical line is truly straight.
* They can perceive the approximate equality of two lines or surfaces, but never exact equality; the same holds for numerical proportions.
* Entities at immense distances cannot be perceived at all, yet geometry treats of extensions produced *ad infinitum*.

If geometry depends in its origin upon sensory data, *how can this transition to perfect exactitude be explained? Or is it perhaps a mere illusion?*

Notice well: in the problem of necessity, we were concerned primarily (if not exclusively) with the nexus between concepts affirmed in judgments. Here a twofold question arises: concerning the **necessary nexus**, but no less concerning the **very existence of the concepts themselves**.

---

#### 2. Historical Perspectives

Philosophers have generally paid little attention to this problem—many, indeed, overlooked it entirely; modern mathematicians, by contrast, have scrutinized it with intense vigor.

##### A) The Philosophers
* **Aristotle** was fully aware of the problem, though he spoke of it sparingly. In *Posterior Analytics* I, 31, he touches upon it, remarking:
  > "Even if it were possible to perceive by sense that a triangle has its angles equal to two right angles, we should still demand a demonstration and not, as some maintain, possess scientific knowledge." [^1]
  
  What commands our attention here is not merely the apodictic requirement of demonstration for universal science, but Aristotle’s clear recognition of the gulf separating sensory data—which can never measure the angle sum with exactitude—from geometric truth manifest to the intellect. 
  
  St. Thomas Aquinas, in his commentary (*In Anal. Post.* I, lect. 42, n. 7), notes that this is *"an example of those things that cannot be perceived by sense."* For the absolute equality between the angle sum of a triangle and two right angles can be perceived by no sense, and attained by no physical measurement.
  
  Consequently, this proposition can never be an immediate intuitive first principle: a direct leap from sensory data to an exact intellective affirmation is here impossible, because empirical inexactitude precludes it. If a transition to exactitude is possible, it must be discovered from other data; and our inquiry is precisely whether such data exist.

* In antiquity, **Proclus** repeatedly emphasized the inexactitude of sensory data; indeed, this was his chief reason for rejecting Aristotle's theory of abstraction in favor of Platonic recollection (*In Euclid. Elem.*, ed. Friedlein, pp. 12, 49, 140). Proclus believed that Aristotelian abstraction could not account for geometric exactitude; hence, while he retained the term *intelligible matter*, he interpreted it in a Platonic sense [^2].

* Among modern philosophers, **George Berkeley and David Hume** failed to distinguish intellectual cognition from sensitive perception (see our *Cosmologia*, 4th ed., pp. 35–36). **Kant**, as noted earlier, failed to formulate the problem of exactitude in its distinctness.

* For **John Stuart Mill**, pursuing the sensationalism of Berkeley and Hume, the problem does not exist, because he denies that geometric exactitude exists at all—either in reality or in the mind. In *A System of Logic* (5th ed., 1862, p. 255), Mill writes:
  > "There exist no points without magnitude; no lines without breadth, nor perfectly straight; no circles with all their radii exactly equal, nor squares with all their angles perfectly right... according to any test we have of possibility, they are not even possible. Their existence, so far as we can form any judgment, would seem to be inconsistent with the physical constitution of our planet at least, if not of the universe...
  > 
  > The points, lines, circles, and squares, which any one has in his mind, are (I apprehend) simply copies of the points, lines, circles, and squares, which he has known in his experience. Our idea of a point, I apprehend to be simply our idea of the *minimum visibile*, the smallest portion of surface which we can see. A line, as defined by geometers, is wholly inconceivable." [^3]
  
  And his conclusion is categorical:
  > "The peculiar accuracy, supposed to be characteristic of the first principles of geometry, thus appears to be fictitious." [^4]

Mill's position is demonstrably false at the outset: for more than twenty centuries, the human mind has clearly distinguished its exact concepts of geometric figures from sensible phantasms, reasoning about them with rigorous intelligibility. Mill fatally confuses distinct species of exactitude.

##### B) The Mathematicians
Modern mathematicians have devoted little attention to the problem of necessity, focusing almost entirely on the problem of exactitude. In their critique, they concluded that **any transition from inexact sensitive data to classical exact geometry is illegitimate**. 

To escape this impasse, they sought to construct mathematical analysis in a **purely arithmetical manner**. Whether, once this analysis is constructed, its subsequent application to extended beings can reconstruct classical geometry remains a serious dilemma.

As noted in Chapter 1, **Felix Klein and Henri Poincaré** formulated this difficulty with great clarity: *"Intuition cannot give us rigor or even certainty,"* Poincaré proclaimed. 

**Eduard Study** (*Die realistische Weltansicht und die Lehre vom Raume*, 1914, pp. 74 sqq.) [^5] declared an analysis-independent classical geometry to be an impossible "utopia," treating geometry as a mere complex of hypotheses whose applicability to reality must be tested experimentally like physical theories.

Perhaps no one formulated this modern objection more pointedly than **J. Wellstein** in the *Weber-Wellstein Enzyklopädie der Elementar-Mathematik* (Vol. II, 3rd ed., 1925, p. 9):
> "The development of modern function theory has demonstrated that the critique of foundations ought to have begun, not at the Fifth Postulate, but immediately at Euclid's very first definition: *A point is that which has no parts* (σημεῖόν ἐστιν οὗ μέρος οὐθέν)." [^6]

Wellstein points out that classical geometry mapped the continuum of real numbers to the points of a geometric line. Modern analysis, fleeing sensory inexactitude, reversed this: it attempted to construct the real numbers purely arithmetically from integers, declaring Euclid's definition of a point to be ungrounded in intuition.

---

#### 3. The Solution to be Sought
If modern mathematicians declare the problem of exactitude insolvable, this cannot erase the undeniable historical and psychological fact: **for over two thousand years, these exact notions have been actively employed by the human mind**. The human intellect abstracted these concepts from extended being as extended, clearly distinguishing them from *minima sensibilia*. The presence of these exact notions in the mind is beyond dispute.

The authentic question is therefore this: **Do there exist real objects—points, lines, surfaces strictly so called—that correspond exactly to these notions?**

Since we are concerned with essences, our primary question is not whether such entities exist as isolated physical bodies in nature, but rather:
* Does extended being as extended admit of **exact boundaries**?
* Can they exist, and in what manner?
* Does *esse* (act of being) belong to them, and how?

Our inquiry resolves into a clear progression:
1. Can objects such as points, lines, and surfaces be discovered within real extended being?
2. Can lines be perfectly straight or circular? Can surfaces be perfectly plane or spherical?
3. Can distinct figures be perfectly congruent, and proportions perfectly determinate?

We must carefully distinguish this mathematical-philosophical question from the cosmological question of whether isolated physical lines or perfect spheres exist in astronomical nature.

---

### § 2. On the Primary Notions of Classical Geometry

#### 1. On the Point, the Line, and the Surface
Wellstein (*op. cit.*, pp. 9–11) argued that exact geometric notions cannot be legitimately derived from sensory experience. If we wish to begin with them, we must—he insists—**postulate the existence of these objects by an arbitrary act of will**:
> "One must remain conscious that this is a pure act of the will, not of the intellect... to the first definition [of Euclid] there belongs in any case a postulate that points exist at all."

Wellstein describes the concept of a point as arising through a **limiting process** (*Grenzprozeß*):
1. We begin with the sensitive image of a grain of sand, imagining it shrinking continually.
2. The imagination soon reaches an absolute halt at the *minimum imaginabile*; beyond that, we are plunged into complete sensory darkness.
3. Because we cannot imagine an end to the process of division, Wellstein claims we must simply **believe or postulate** that a limit exists beyond which division cannot go.
4. Hence, Euclid’s primary definition would be the offspring of an act of *will*, not of *intellective insight*.

This conclusion would spell the ruin of geometry as a science of reality. But Wellstein’s premise is false. If shrinking grains of sand were the only way to arrive at a point, Wellstein would be correct. But we possess **another, authentic intellective intuition of indivisible boundaries**, which renders the limiting process legitimate without any arbitrary postulation.

---

#### 2. The Division of Extended Being

To discover this intellective insight, we do not begin with a microscopic point, but with the primary datum of all spatial experience: **extended being as extended** (*ens extensum ut extensum*)—a solid body possessing three dimensions.

##### The Discovery of the Indivisible Boundary
1. The primary proper attribute (*proprium*) that the intellect detects in extended being is its **divisibility**.
2. Let such an extension be divided into two parts—merely divided, without separating or moving the parts.
3. Between these two parts there exists a **boundary** (*limes*).
4. Through this boundary, transition can be made from one part to the other.
5. And here is the decisive insight of the intellect: **the transition from one part to the other occurs in an INDIVISIBLE!**

This indivisibility is not perceived by the imagination (which can grasp only extended magnitudes), but is **luminous to the intellect**:
* The boundary stands in a twofold relation to the parts it divides:
  * It lies **alongside** (*iuxta*) the parts: in this respect it is extended and divisible.
  * It lies **between** (*inter*) the parts: in this respect, as that through which transition occurs, **it is absolutely indivisible!**
* If the boundary between two parts possessed thickness—if it had extension *between* the parts—the original body would be divided not into two parts, but into **three parts** (the two parts plus the boundary layer); and for each of these boundaries the same question would recur *ad infinitum*. The body would not be divisible into two parts!

##### A) The Surface
The boundary between two parts of a three-dimensional solid is a **surface** [^7]. It is defined as:
$$\text{"That through which transition is made from one part of a solid to the other."}$$
Precisely under the aspect in which transition occurs, **it is absolutely indivisible**—it has zero depth.

Here, in the sensible phantasm, the intellect discovers the **first exact indivisible** (the first exact geometric concept). It attains both the *necessity* and the *exactitude* of this first geometric principle, even though neither necessity nor exactitude exists in sensitive perception. Under both respects, **the intellect transcends sense**.

The surface is thus:
* Indivisible in depth (*inter partes*);
* Divisible and extended in length and breadth (*iuxta partes*).

##### B) The Line and the Point
The exact same intellective analysis applies to the division of the surface:
1. Divide the surface into two parts: the boundary between them is that through which transition occurs—it is **indivisible in breadth**. This indivisible boundary is the **line**. The line is extended in length (*iuxta partes*), but indivisible in breadth (*inter partes*).
2. Divide the line: the boundary dividing a line into two parts is that through which transition occurs—it is **indivisible in length**.
3. Here the process reaches an absolute and natural termination: we arrive at that which is **indivisible in every dimension**—the geometric **point**, precisely as described by Euclid:
$$\text{σημεῖόν ἐστιν οὗ μέρος οὐθέν} \quad \text{("A point is that which has no parts")}$$

A point is not a nothingness (*nihil*): it is the boundary of a line, which is the boundary of a surface, which is the boundary of a solid extended being given directly in sensory experience. 

Because it is a boundary, it is an **indivisible possessing position** (*indivisibile positionem habens*)—the classic definition of Aristotle and St. Thomas (*Post. Anal.* I, 27; *Metaph.* V, 6).

##### Mathematical vs. Physical Existence
* **Mathematical Existence**: These boundaries exist **in potency** within undivided extension; they exist **in act** upon division. Their mathematical existence is deduced with necessity from the nature of extended being.
* **Physical Existence in Nature**: 
  * Real physical bodies possess actual surfaces bounding them from the surrounding medium. Even if these surfaces are microscopically rough, the transition from body to non-body occurs in an indivisible.
  * Do physical lines exist in act in nature? If a body has bounded surfaces (facets, edges), the boundaries of those surfaces are real physical lines. If a body were a seamless sphere, its surface would be finite yet unbounded, containing lines only in potency.
  * Points exist in act wherever edges intersect (such as the vertices of a crystal).

---

#### 3. On Dimensions
The successive application of division reveals the nature of **dimensions**:
* The process of boundary-division can be applied to extended being **precisely three times**:
  1. Solid (3D) $\xrightarrow{\text{division}}$ Surface (2D boundary);
  2. Surface (2D) $\xrightarrow{\text{division}}$ Line (1D boundary);
  3. Line (1D) $\xrightarrow{\text{division}}$ Point (0D boundary).
* The point can undergo no further division: it is indivisible in all respects.

While "dimension" cannot be strictly defined by generic and specific difference (being a primitive notion), it is rendered completely transparent through this **operative definition**:
> A **three-dimensional being** is an extended being that admits of this threefold boundary-division.

*Can there exist an extended being of two-and-a-half dimensions?* The question is meaningless—and the immediacy with which we recognize its absurdity demonstrates the clarity of the concept.

*Can entities of 1 or 2 dimensions exist independently as autonomous substances?* We know them only as boundaries of 3D bodies. Whether they could exist separately in nature is a question concerning which we must confess complete ignorance. 

*Can a four-dimensional being exist, bounded by a 3D solid?* We lack any positive experiential or intuitive ground to affirm its possibility (see *Cosmologia*, 4th ed., pp. 471–474).

---

#### 4. The Objection Drawn from the Möbius Strip

Certain modern authors raised what they considered a fatal objection against defining a surface as the boundary of a solid. 

Louis Couturat (*Revue de Métaphysique et de Morale*, 1904, p. 810) asserted:
> "Certain authors define a surface as that which bounds a solid. But there exist certain surfaces that have only one side, or whose two sides connect in a continuous manner, so that they do not divide space into two separate regions, and consequently cannot serve to delimit a solid." [^8]

Wellstein (*op. cit.*, p. 10) leveled the same objection:
> "To take one's departure in forming the concept of a surface merely from the outer surface or from that which is common to two bodies is insufficient, because there exist surfaces that cannot, as a whole, bound a body." [^9]

##### Hoenen’s Refutation
These authors err, and their objection is resolved on two distinct levels:

1. **The Local Resolution (Aurel Voss)**: To verify the nature of a surface as an indivisible boundary, it suffices to take **any local patch** of a Möbius strip (hold the paper strip between two fingers: the surface cleanly separates the two fingers as an indivisible boundary). As A. Voss observed (*Die Kultur der Gegenwart* III, p. E 96):
   > "There exist indeed unilateral surfaces that bound no part of space; but this property belongs to them only on account of their special global connectivity, while their elementary parts preserve the indicated character." [^10]

2. **The Global Topological Discovery**: Take a physical model of a Möbius strip constructed from paper or rubber. It possesses a finite thickness (depth). It is therefore a **three-dimensional solid**!
   * What bounds this physical strip? It is bounded by **two distinct Möbius surfaces**: the wide twisted face and the narrow edge-ribbon! 
   * We have a real solid body completely bounded by two Möbius surfaces, perfectly separated from the surrounding space.
   * If constructed from a thick rubber beam, it becomes obvious that the wide face has **two sides**: one facing the outer surrounding space, and one facing the interior of the rubber solid! The claim that it cannot bound a body is an elementary physical and geometric error.

#### 5. Consequences for General Epistemology

##### A) Aristotelian Abstraction Vindicated
* In extended being as extended, indivisible boundaries exist necessarily.
* This exactitude is not found in sensitive perception, where boundaries are always blurred and thick.
* The exact judgment is affirmed **not of the phantasm, but of the extended being in reality**.
* The senses provide the *occasion and matter* of the cognition; the **intellectus agens** penetrates the phantasm to extract the exact intelligible species:
  > *"Sensory cognition is not the total and perfect cause of intellectual cognition, but rather in a certain manner the matter of the cause... hence it is no wonder if intellectual cognition extends beyond sensitive cognition."* (St. Thomas, *S. Th.* I, q. 84, a. 6).

As St. Thomas observes (*S. Th.* III, q. 11, a. 2, ad 1):
> "The end of the visual power is to know colors; but the end of the intellective power is not to know phantasms, but to know intelligible species [^11] which it apprehends from phantasms and in phantasms according to the state of the present life."

##### B) Refutation of Rival Epistemologies
* **Plato**: Senses do not merely evoke recollection; the intellect actively reads real boundaries in the phantasm.
* **Kant**: Extension cannot be a mere subjective form of inner/outer sensibility. If extension existed only in phenomenal sensibility, geometry would have no application: not to the *thing-in-itself* (which lacks extension), nor to the *phenomenon* (which lacks exactitude).
* **Mill**: Indivisible points and widthless lines are neither fictions nor copies of *minima visibilia*; they are exact intelligible boundaries whose mathematical existence is demonstrable.

#### 6. On Infinite Divisibility

##### A) Divisibility into Extended Parts
Divisibility flows necessarily from extension:
1. Every extended magnitude can be divided into two smaller extended magnitudes.
2. The resulting parts are themselves extended; hence they too are divisible into smaller extensions: **ad infinitum**.
3. The imagination reaches its threshold at the *minimum imaginabile*; but the intellect grasps that even the *minimum imaginabile* is extended, and therefore divisible.

##### Can an Extension be Divided Exhaustively into Points?
Could a single, instantaneous cut divide an extension so exhaustively as to leave *only indivisible points*? **No.**
* The parts into which a whole is divided must reconstitute the whole upon addition.
* Points have zero extension ($0$ length). Adding zeroes of extension cannot produce extension:
$$\text{Point} + \text{Point} + \dots = 0 \text{ extension}$$
* Geometric addition applies only to quantity added to quantity.
* Therefore, **a continuum cannot be composed of indivisible points, nor divided exhaustively into points**. Every division of an extension yields smaller extensions: it is **infinitely divisible** (Aristotle, *Physics* VI).

##### B) The Line as a "Locus" vs. a "Collection" of Points
* In classical geometry, a line is described as the **locus of points** satisfying a given property (e.g., the angle bisector as the locus of points equidistant from two rays). This does not mean the line is built up by assembling points; rather, the line is the **matrix and potentiality** in which equidistant points can be actualized.
* In **Cantor’s early set theory**, a line was conceived as an actualized infinite collection of discrete points. This naive view generated foundational paradoxes.
* As **L. E. J. Brouwer** (*Over de grondslagen der wiskunde*, 1907, p. 8) correctly noted: **a line is a matrix of points to be thought together**—an Aristotelian potentiality that is actualized by further determination.
* As **Henri Poincaré** famously declared (*Science et Méthode*, p. 212):
  > *"There is no actual infinity; the Cantoriens forgot this, and they fell into contradiction."*

##### C) Analytical Geometry and Continuous Motion
An equation such as $y = \sin x$ does not generate a line by static compilation of coordinate pairs. A line is generated geometrically through **continuous kinematic motion**:
$$x = t, \quad y = \sin t$$
The abscissa moves uniformly ($x = t$) while the ordinate oscillates periodically ($y = \sin t$). The resulting curve is continuous because the underlying physical/spatial motion is continuous.

##### The Poincaré Objection: Non-Differentiable Curves
Poincaré (*La valeur de la science*, pp. 17 sq.) argued that geometric intuition is discredited because analysis discovered continuous functions that possess no derivative (no tangent line), such as:
$$y = x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \quad \text{with } y(0) = 0$$
Toward the origin, the oscillations compress infinitely. Poincaré claimed that intuition falsely leads us to believe that every continuous curve must have a tangent.

Hoenen provides a brilliant mathematical-philosophical refutation:
* Does the curve $y = x \sin(1/x)$ **geometrically exist** as a complete curve at the origin?
* If we construct it kinematically:
$$x = a - t, \quad y = (a - t) \sin\left(\frac{1}{a - t}\right)$$
To reach $t = a$ (the origin), the generating point would have to execute an **actual infinity of oscillations in finite time**! 
* But by definition, an actual infinity cannot be traversed (*infinitum pertransiri nequit*).
* Hence, the kinematic motion cannot reach the origin under that law; the curve does not exist geometrically as a completed entity across $x = 0$. Anywhere away from zero, the curve exists and **possesses a tangent everywhere**.
* Pathological "nowhere-differentiable" curves (such as Weierstrass curves) are analytical limits, not intuitable geometric curves. Intuition is fully vindicated.

---

#### 7. Geometry as a Constructive Science

Kant correctly identified geometry as a **constructive science**: geometric objects are constructed in intuition.

However, modern idealists and formalists exaggerated this insight into an error, claiming that geometry is an unconstrained **free creation of the human mind** (*creatio ex nihilo*), severing it from reality.

Hoenen restores the classical balance:
1. We construct in the mind, but we construct from **pre-existing intelligible matter**—extended being as extended.
2. We do not arbitrarily invent; we **discover** that extended being is a fit subject for construction:
   * We discover **constructibility** as a proper attribute of extension;
   * We discover that multitude arises from division;
   * We discover exact boundaries and points existing in potency.
3. Our constructive activity is bound by the nature of the matter:
   > *"Sensory data are not the total cause, but the matter of the cause."*

Geometric construction is the **intellective actualization of real potentialities in intelligible matter**. The connection between geometry and physical reality is never severed.
